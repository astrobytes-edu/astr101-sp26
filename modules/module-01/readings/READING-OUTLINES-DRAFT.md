# Week 3: Gravity & Orbits (Feb 2-6)

## Lecture 5: Gravity — The Universal Force

### Big Idea
> Gravity is the cosmic glue. It sculpts everything from falling apples to orbiting planets, and — crucially for astronomers — **orbital motion reveals mass**.

### Learning Objectives
- State Newton's law of universal gravitation in words and recognize the equation
- Explain why gravity is an "inverse-square law" and what that means physically
- Connect gravitational acceleration to free-fall and weightlessness
- Explain why astronauts float (it's NOT "no gravity")
- Describe how orbital motion can reveal the mass of an unseen object

### Outline

#### 1. Opening Hook: The Same Force
- Newton's insight: the force that makes apples fall is the same force that keeps the Moon in orbit
- This was revolutionary — celestial and terrestrial physics unified
- **Check Yourself:** What did Newton unify that no one before had connected?

#### 2. The Law of Gravity
- Every mass attracts every other mass
- The equation: F = GMm/r² (explain each term in words)
- **Inverse-square law:** double the distance → quarter the force
- Analogy: light from a flashlight spreading out
- **Margin definition:** Inverse-square law
- **Check Yourself:** If you move twice as far from Earth, gravity becomes... (1/4 as strong)

#### 3. Gravitational Acceleration
- Near Earth's surface: g ≈ 9.8 m/s²
- All objects fall at the same rate (Galileo's insight, Apollo 15 hammer-feather demo)
- **Misconception bust:** Heavy objects do NOT fall faster
- **Check Yourself:** On the Moon (weaker gravity), a hammer and feather dropped together will... (still hit at same time)

#### 4. Why Astronauts Float
- **Misconception bust:** Astronauts are NOT in "zero gravity"
- At ISS altitude (~400 km), g is still ~90% of surface value
- They float because they're in **free fall** — continuously falling *around* Earth
- Weightlessness = falling with no floor to push back
- **Check Yourself:** An astronaut on the ISS experiences approximately what fraction of Earth's surface gravity? (about 90%)

#### 5. Orbits: Falling and Missing
- An orbit is perpetual free fall — falling toward Earth but moving sideways fast enough to miss
- Newton's cannon thought experiment
- Circular orbit: constant speed, constant altitude, gravity = centripetal force
- Elliptical orbits: speed varies (faster near perihelion)
- **Demo connection:** [Kepler's Laws demo] for visualization

#### 6. **The Astronomer's Power Tool: Orbits Reveal Mass**
- This is the payoff for all of astronomy
- If we can measure an orbit (period + size), we can calculate mass
- Example: Earth's mass from the Moon's orbit
- Example: Sun's mass from Earth's orbit
- **Connection to L1:** Mass is NOT a direct observable — we infer it from motion
- **Check Yourself:** How do astronomers "weigh" the Sun? (By measuring Earth's orbital motion)

#### 7. Deep Dive (collapsible): Deriving Orbital Velocity
- v = √(GM/r)
- For a circular orbit, set gravitational force = centripetal force
- Show the algebra step by step
- Physical interpretation: farther orbits are slower

#### 8. Practice Problems
- Calculate orbital speed of ISS
- Compare gravitational force at different distances
- Conceptual: why don't satellites fall down?

## Lecture 6: Kepler's Laws — The Grammar of Orbits

### Big Idea
> Kepler discovered that planets follow ellipses, not circles — and encoded three empirical rules that Newton later explained with gravity. These laws are the astronomer's toolkit for decoding any orbit.

### Learning Objectives
- State Kepler's three laws of planetary motion
- Distinguish ellipses from circles and define key terms (focus, semi-major axis, eccentricity)
- Use Kepler's 3rd Law to relate orbital period and distance
- Explain why Kepler's laws work for any orbiting system (not just planets)

### Outline

#### 1. Opening: Kepler's Quest
- Tycho Brahe's precise data + Kepler's mathematical persistence
- Kepler tried circles for years before accepting ellipses
- Historical context: this was data-driven discovery before "scientific method" was named
- **Check Yourself:** Kepler discovered his laws by... (analyzing Tycho's observational data)

#### 2. Kepler's First Law: Ellipses
- Planets orbit the Sun in ellipses with the Sun at one focus
- Define: ellipse, focus, semi-major axis (a), eccentricity (e)
- Earth's orbit is nearly circular (e ≈ 0.017)
- Mercury and comets are more eccentric
- **Sketch It:** Draw an ellipse with both foci, label the semi-major axis
- **Margin definition:** Semi-major axis, Eccentricity
- **Check Yourself:** An orbit with eccentricity = 0 is... (a circle)

#### 3. Kepler's Second Law: Equal Areas
- A line from Sun to planet sweeps equal areas in equal times
- Physical consequence: planets move **faster when closer** to the Sun
- This is conservation of angular momentum (don't need to derive, just state)
- **Demo connection:** [Kepler's Laws demo] — watch the area sweeping
- **Check Yourself:** Earth moves fastest in its orbit during which month? (January — perihelion)

#### 4. Kepler's Third Law: The Period-Distance Relation
- P² ∝ a³ (in appropriate units)
- For objects orbiting the Sun: P² = a³ (P in years, a in AU)
- **This is the power law:** farther planets orbit slower, but not linearly
- Table: Solar system planets showing P² = a³
- **Check Yourself:** A planet at 4 AU would have an orbital period of... (8 years)

#### 5. Newton's Generalization
- Kepler's laws are consequences of gravity
- The full form: P² = (4π²/GM) a³
- **Key insight:** The mass M appears — so we can measure mass from orbits!
- Works for any two-body system: binary stars, exoplanets, moons, black holes
- **Check Yourself:** Kepler's 3rd Law can be used to determine the mass of... (the object being orbited)

#### 6. Deep Dive (collapsible): Deriving Kepler's 3rd Law
- Start from circular orbit: v = 2πa/P
- Set gravitational force = centripetal force
- Solve for P
- Show that M appears in the denominator

#### 7. Application: Weighing the Milky Way
- Stars orbit the galactic center
- Measuring their speeds and distances lets us infer the enclosed mass
- Spoiler: we find way more mass than we can see → dark matter
- **Connection to L1:** Pretty pictures → measurements → models → inferences

#### 8. Practice Problems
- Use Kepler's 3rd Law to find orbital period from distance
- Given period and distance, calculate central mass
- Conceptual: why does Jupiter have more moons than Earth?

# Week 4: Light & Radiation (Feb 9-13)

## Lecture 7: Light — The Cosmic Messenger

### Big Idea
> Light is the only messenger from the cosmos that reaches us directly. Understanding what light *is* — electromagnetic waves carrying energy — unlocks everything astronomers do.

### Learning Objectives
- Describe light as electromagnetic waves with wavelength, frequency, and speed
- State that all EM waves travel at c ≈ 3×10⁸ m/s in vacuum
- Relate wavelength and frequency using c = λf
- Identify regions of the EM spectrum and what they reveal about different phenomena

### Outline

#### 1. Opening: Light Is All We Have
- Callback to L1: we can't visit stars, can't collect samples
- Light (broadly: electromagnetic radiation) is our only direct connection
- Every piece of astronomical knowledge comes from decoding light
- **Check Yourself:** What is the only way astronomers can study distant stars? (by analyzing the light they emit)

#### 2. What Is Light?
- Light is an electromagnetic wave — oscillating electric and magnetic fields
- Wave properties: wavelength (λ), frequency (f), speed (c)
- c = λf — know this relationship
- In vacuum, all EM waves travel at c ≈ 3×10⁸ m/s
- **Margin definition:** Wavelength, Frequency
- **Check Yourself:** If a wave has a longer wavelength, its frequency is... (lower)

#### 3. The Electromagnetic Spectrum
- Radio → Microwave → Infrared → Visible → Ultraviolet → X-ray → Gamma ray
- Visible light is a tiny sliver (400-700 nm)
- Different wavelengths reveal different physics
  - Radio: cold gas, magnetic fields
  - Infrared: warm dust, star-forming regions
  - Visible: stellar surfaces
  - UV/X-ray: hot gas, violent events
  - Gamma: most extreme energy processes
- **Figure:** EM spectrum with astronomical sources at each wavelength
- **Check Yourself:** Which type of light would you use to study cold molecular clouds? (radio/infrared)

#### 4. Earth's Atmosphere: A Filter
- Atmosphere blocks most EM radiation
- Only "windows" in visible and radio
- Why we need space telescopes (Hubble, JWST, Chandra)
- **Figure:** Atmospheric transmission vs. wavelength
- **Check Yourself:** Why is JWST in space? (to detect infrared light that Earth's atmosphere absorbs)

#### 5. Light as Particles: Photons
- Light also behaves as particles (photons)
- Energy of a photon: E = hf (higher frequency = higher energy)
- Detectors count photons
- **Margin definition:** Photon
- **Check Yourself:** Which has more energy per photon — red light or blue light? (blue)

#### 6. The Speed of Light and Lookback Time
- Light travels at finite speed → we see the past
- 1 light-year = distance light travels in one year
- Sun: 8 minutes ago; nearest star: 4 years ago; Andromeda: 2.5 million years ago
- Astronomy is cosmic archaeology
- **Check Yourself:** When we observe a galaxy 100 million light-years away, we see it as it was... (100 million years ago)

#### 7. Deep Dive (collapsible): Wave Interference and Diffraction
- Constructive and destructive interference
- Diffraction through apertures
- Why telescope resolution depends on wavelength/diameter
- [Brief — connects to telescope lecture]

#### 8. Practice Problems
- Calculate frequency from wavelength
- Order EM types by energy
- Conceptual: why do radio telescopes need to be so large?

## Lecture 8: Blackbody Radiation — Temperature Has a Color

### Big Idea
> Hot objects glow. The color and intensity of that glow encode the object's temperature. This "blackbody radiation" lets astronomers take the temperature of stars from billions of kilometers away.

### Learning Objectives
- Define blackbody radiation and explain why all hot objects emit it
- State Wien's Law (peak wavelength shifts with temperature) and use it
- State the Stefan-Boltzmann Law (luminosity increases rapidly with temperature)
- Explain how astronomers determine stellar temperatures from color

### Outline

#### 1. Opening: Why Stars Have Colors
- Betelgeuse is red, Rigel is blue — why?
- It's not composition (like paint) — it's temperature
- **Misconception bust:** Stellar color ≠ chemical composition (that's spectral lines)
- **Check Yourself:** A red star is ______ than a blue star. (cooler)

#### 2. What Is a Blackbody?
- Ideal emitter/absorber of radiation
- Emits a continuous spectrum that depends ONLY on temperature
- Stars approximate blackbodies reasonably well
- **Margin definition:** Blackbody
- **Demo connection:** [Blackbody Radiation demo]

#### 3. The Blackbody Spectrum
- Characteristic curve: rises, peaks, falls
- Higher temperature → peak shifts to shorter wavelengths
- Higher temperature → emits more energy at ALL wavelengths
- **Figure:** Blackbody curves at different temperatures (labeled with star examples)
- **Check Yourself:** As temperature increases, the peak wavelength... (decreases / shifts bluer)

#### 4. Wien's Law: Temperature Sets the Color
- λ_peak = 2.9×10⁶ nm·K / T
- Hotter → bluer peak; cooler → redder peak
- Example: Sun at 5800 K → peak ~500 nm (green-yellow visible)
- Example: Human body at 310 K → peak ~9400 nm (infrared — we glow in IR!)
- **Check Yourself:** A star with surface temperature 3000 K peaks at what wavelength? (~970 nm, infrared)

#### 5. Stefan-Boltzmann Law: Temperature Sets the Power
- L = σAT⁴ (luminosity = constant × surface area × temperature⁴)
- Temperature matters A LOT — double T, multiply luminosity by 16
- This is why hot stars are so much more luminous
- **Check Yourself:** If a star's temperature doubles, its luminosity increases by a factor of... (16)

#### 6. Putting It Together: Measuring Star Temperatures
- Measure the star's color (compare brightness at different wavelengths)
- Fit a blackbody curve → get temperature
- This works from billions of km away!
- **Connection to L1:** Color (wavelength) is a direct observable; temperature is inferred
- **Check Yourself:** Astronomers determine a star's temperature by measuring its... (color/spectrum)

#### 7. Deep Dive (collapsible): The Ultraviolet Catastrophe
- Classical physics predicted infinite energy at short wavelengths
- Planck's quantum hypothesis fixed it (E = hf)
- Birth of quantum mechanics from an astrophysical problem

#### 8. Practice Problems
- Use Wien's Law to find peak wavelength
- Compare luminosities of stars with different temperatures
- Conceptual: why can't you see your body glow?

# Week 5: Spectra & Telescopes (Feb 17-20)

*Note: Monday Feb 16 is Presidents Day — no class. This week has only 2 lecture days.*

## Lecture 9: Spectra — Fingerprints of Atoms

### Big Idea
> Every element produces a unique pattern of spectral lines — a barcode that identifies its presence. Spectroscopy transforms a simple brightness measurement into a goldmine of information about composition, temperature, and motion.

### Learning Objectives
- Distinguish continuous, emission, and absorption spectra
- Explain how atomic energy levels produce specific wavelengths (qualitatively)
- Identify that each element has a unique spectral "fingerprint"
- Describe how spectral lines reveal composition and physical conditions
- Explain the Doppler effect and how it reveals motion toward/away from us

### Outline

#### 1. Opening: Beyond Color
- L8 gave us temperature from overall color
- But there's more information hiding in the light
- Spread light into a spectrum → see bright and dark lines
- These lines are the fingerprints of atoms
- **Check Yourself:** Spectral lines tell us about a star's... (composition and motion)

#### 2. Three Types of Spectra
- **Continuous spectrum:** hot dense object (blackbody)
- **Emission spectrum:** bright lines on dark background (hot thin gas)
- **Absorption spectrum:** dark lines on continuous background (cool gas in front of hot source)
- Kirchhoff's Laws (qualitative description)
- **Figure:** All three types side by side
- **Margin definitions:** Continuous, Emission, Absorption spectrum
- **Check Yourself:** A hot thin gas produces what type of spectrum? (emission)

#### 3. Atomic Energy Levels (Qualitative)
- Electrons in atoms exist in specific energy "rungs"
- Photon absorption: electron jumps up (absorbs specific wavelength)
- Photon emission: electron jumps down (emits specific wavelength)
- Each element has unique energy levels → unique line pattern
- Hydrogen: Balmer series (visible lines at 656 nm, 486 nm, etc.)
- **Figure:** Energy level diagram for hydrogen
- **Do NOT need:** Quantum numbers, orbital shapes, Bohr model math
- **Check Yourself:** Why does each element have a unique spectral fingerprint? (unique energy levels)

#### 4. Reading a Stellar Spectrum
- Stars show absorption spectra (hot interior, cooler atmosphere)
- Which lines appear → which elements are present
- Line strength → how much of each element
- Example: Solar spectrum with labeled lines (H, Ca, Na, Fe...)
- **Connection to L1:** Wavelength is observable; composition is inferred
- **Check Yourself:** Dark lines in a stellar spectrum are caused by... (absorption by cooler gas in the star's atmosphere)

#### 5. The Doppler Effect: Motion from Wavelength
- Approaching source: wavelength compressed → blueshift
- Receding source: wavelength stretched → redshift
- Δλ/λ = v/c (for slow speeds)
- Measure the shift of known spectral lines → calculate velocity
- Works for stars, galaxies, exoplanets
- **Figure:** Doppler shift diagram
- **Margin definition:** Redshift, Blueshift
- **Check Yourself:** A star moving toward us shows spectral lines that are... (blueshifted)

#### 6. Doppler Applications
- Binary star orbits (radial velocity curves)
- Exoplanet detection (star wobble)
- Galaxy rotation (one side approaching, one receding)
- Cosmic expansion (galaxy redshifts → Hubble's Law preview)
- **Check Yourself:** How do astronomers detect exoplanets using the Doppler effect? (by measuring the star's wobble)

#### 7. Deep Dive (collapsible): The Doppler Formula
- v = c × Δλ/λ₀
- Example calculation with hydrogen line
- Relativistic correction for very high speeds (just mention, don't derive)

#### 8. Practice Problems
- Identify spectrum type from description
- Calculate Doppler velocity from line shift
- Conceptual: why are spectral lines called "fingerprints"?

## Lecture 10: Telescopes — Gathering Light

### Big Idea
> Telescopes are light buckets. Their primary job is to collect more photons than our eyes can, enabling us to see fainter and farther. Resolution — the ability to see fine detail — comes second.

### Learning Objectives
- Explain that telescopes primarily gather light (not magnify)
- Describe how larger aperture enables fainter object detection
- Explain diffraction-limited resolution and why larger is better
- Compare reflecting and refracting telescope designs
- Describe why astronomers use space telescopes and different wavelength facilities

### Outline

#### 1. Opening: Why Build Telescopes?
- **Misconception bust:** Telescopes are NOT mainly about magnification
- Primary purpose: gather more light (larger "light bucket")
- Secondary: resolve finer details
- Our eyes have tiny pupils (~5mm); telescopes can be meters across
- **Check Yourself:** The main advantage of a large telescope is... (it collects more light)

#### 2. Light-Gathering Power
- Collecting area = π(D/2)² — scales with diameter squared
- 10 m telescope collects 4× more light than 5 m telescope
- More photons → can see fainter objects
- Can also reduce exposure time for brighter objects
- **Demo connection:** [Telescope Resolution demo]
- **Check Yourself:** A telescope with twice the diameter collects... times more light. (4)

#### 3. Angular Resolution
- Ability to distinguish two close objects
- Limited by diffraction: θ ∝ λ/D
- Larger telescope → better resolution
- Shorter wavelength → better resolution (why optical beats radio for detail)
- **Margin definition:** Angular resolution, Diffraction limit
- **Check Yourself:** To improve resolution, you should use a ______ telescope at ______ wavelengths. (larger, shorter)

#### 4. Refractors vs. Reflectors
- Refracting telescope: lens focuses light
- Reflecting telescope: mirror focuses light
- All large modern telescopes are reflectors (why: weight, chromatic aberration, cost)
- Different mirror shapes for different purposes
- **Figure:** Optical paths for both types
- **Check Yourself:** Why are all large modern telescopes reflectors, not refractors? (mirrors don't have chromatic aberration and are easier to support)

#### 5. Beating Atmospheric Blur
- Earth's atmosphere causes "twinkling" (seeing)
- Limits ground-based resolution to ~1 arcsecond typically
- Solutions:
  - Space telescopes (above atmosphere)
  - Adaptive optics (deformable mirrors correct for turbulence)
  - Interferometry (combine multiple telescopes)
- **Check Yourself:** "Twinkling" of stars is caused by... (Earth's atmospheric turbulence)

#### 6. Multi-Wavelength Astronomy
- Different telescopes for different wavelengths
- Radio: large dishes, interferometers (VLA, ALMA)
- Infrared: must be cold, often in space (JWST)
- Optical: ground and space (Keck, HST)
- X-ray/Gamma: space only (Chandra, Fermi)
- **Figure:** Multi-wavelength view of a galaxy
- **Check Yourself:** Why must X-ray telescopes be in space? (Earth's atmosphere absorbs X-rays)

#### 7. Deep Dive (collapsible): Diffraction Limit Math
- θ = 1.22 λ/D (in radians)
- Example calculation for HST vs. human eye
- Why radio telescopes need to be huge

#### 8. Practice Problems
- Compare light-gathering power of two telescopes
- Calculate resolution from diameter and wavelength
- Conceptual: why do we need both space and ground-based telescopes?

# Week 6: Solar System (Feb 23-27)

*Note: Friday is review for Module 1 Exam (Mon Mar 2)*

## Lecture 11: The Solar System — Our Cosmic Backyard

### Big Idea
> The Solar System is a laboratory where we can apply everything we've learned — gravity, light, spectra — to objects we can visit. Its structure (rocky inner, gaseous outer, icy beyond) records the story of how planetary systems form.

### Learning Objectives
- Describe the basic structure of the Solar System (terrestrial, jovian, trans-Neptunian)
- Explain how the "frost line" shaped planetary composition
- Apply Kepler's Laws and Newton's gravity to Solar System dynamics
- Describe how we've explored the Solar System (flybys, orbiters, landers, rovers)

### Outline

#### 1. Opening: Why the Solar System Matters
- It's the one planetary system we can study up close
- Spacecraft have visited every planet
- Testing ground for physics we've learned
- **Connection to course:** All the tools from Weeks 1-5 apply here
- **Check Yourself:** Why is the Solar System especially valuable for astronomers? (we can visit it and study it directly)

#### 2. A Tour in Numbers
- Sun: 99.8% of Solar System mass
- 8 planets, ~200 moons, millions of small bodies
- Orbital distances: Mercury (0.4 AU) to Neptune (30 AU)
- Kepler's 3rd Law in action: Neptune's 165-year orbit
- **Figure:** Solar System to scale (planets are tiny compared to distances)
- **Check Yourself:** Which object contains most of the Solar System's mass? (the Sun)

#### 3. Two Families of Planets
- **Terrestrial planets:** Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars
  - Rocky, dense, small, few or no moons, close to Sun
- **Jovian planets:** Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune
  - Gaseous, low density, large, many moons, far from Sun
- Why the difference? The **frost line** (next section)
- **Check Yourself:** Terrestrial planets are made mostly of... (rock and metal)

#### 4. The Frost Line: Temperature Shapes Composition
- Early Solar System: hot near Sun, cold far from Sun
- Frost line: ~3 AU, where water ice could condense
- Inside frost line: only rock and metal survived → small rocky planets
- Outside frost line: ice + rock + captured gas → giant planets
- This explains the terrestrial/jovian divide
- **Margin definition:** Frost line
- **Check Yourself:** Giant planets formed beyond the frost line because... (ices could condense there, providing more mass to capture gas)

#### 5. Applying What We've Learned
- **Gravity:** planetary orbits, moon systems, spacecraft trajectories
- **Kepler's Laws:** predict where planets will be, design missions
- **Spectra:** identify atmospheric composition remotely
- **Doppler:** measure rotation, wind speeds
- Example: We measured Jupiter's atmospheric composition from Earth before Galileo arrived
- **Check Yourself:** Astronomers identified Jupiter's atmosphere composition by... (analyzing spectral lines in its reflected light)

#### 6. Small Bodies: Leftovers from Formation
- Asteroids (rocky): mainly in asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter
- Comets (icy): from outer Solar System (Kuiper Belt, Oort Cloud)
- Why they matter: pristine samples of early Solar System
- Brief mention: planetary defense (Chelyabinsk, DART mission)
- **Check Yourself:** Comets are made mostly of... (ice and dust)

#### 7. Exploration Highlights
- Flybys, orbiters, landers, rovers, sample return
- Key missions: Voyager, Cassini, Mars rovers, New Horizons
- Each mission = applying physics + engineering
- Don't need to memorize missions — appreciate the progression
- **Check Yourself:** What was significant about the Voyager missions? (first to fly by outer planets)

#### 8. Deep Dive (collapsible): Planetary Differentiation
- How terrestrial planets separated into core/mantle/crust
- Density and the sinking of heavy elements
- Why Earth has a magnetic field (liquid iron core)

#### 9. Practice Problems
- Use Kepler's 3rd Law for a planet's orbit
- Classify planets as terrestrial or jovian
- Conceptual: why are there no large rocky planets beyond the frost line?
