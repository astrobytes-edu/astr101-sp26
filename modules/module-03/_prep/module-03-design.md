## Narrative Arc: "Zooming Out — The Universe as a Whole"

Module 2 told the life story of individual stars. Module 3 zooms out: stars are
organized into galaxies, galaxies are organized into a web-like cosmic structure, and
the whole thing is expanding from a hot, dense beginning.

The arc follows the distance ladder outward — from the Milky Way to the observable
universe — and culminates in the biggest questions in science: How did the universe
begin? What is it made of? What is its fate?

**Throughline question:** *"How do we know the universe had a beginning, and what will
happen next?"*

**Capstone insight:** The same physics that governs individual stars (gravity, light,
spectra) explains the structure and evolution of the entire cosmos — but 95% of the
universe is made of stuff we can't see.

## Observable → Model → Inference Map

| Observable | Model / Physical Law | Inference |
|-----------|---------------------|-----------|
| Star counts + distributions | Galactic structure models | Milky Way shape, extent |
| Cepheid period-luminosity | Stellar pulsation physics | Galaxy distances |
| Stellar orbital velocities vs. radius | Newtonian gravity, mass within orbit | Dark matter (rotation curves) |
| Galaxy redshifts | Doppler effect, cosmological expansion | Recession velocity, distance (Hubble's law) |
| Gravitational lensing arcs | General relativity | Mass of lens (mostly dark matter) |
| Type Ia SN brightness at high redshift | Standard candle + expansion model | Accelerating expansion → dark energy |
| Cosmic microwave background (CMB) | Big Bang cosmology, blackbody at 2.7 K | Age, composition, geometry of universe |

## Lecture-by-Lecture Outline

### L19 — The Milky Way

**Date:** Monday, April 20
**Big Idea:** We live inside a galaxy — a vast disk of 200+ billion stars — and mapping
it from the inside out is one of astronomy's great detective stories.

**Learning Objectives (Bloom's):**

1. Describe the major structural components of the Milky Way: disk, bulge, halo, spiral arms (Remember)
2. Explain how astronomers determined the Sun's position and the Milky Way's size using Cepheid variables and other tracers (Understand)
3. Describe the evidence for a supermassive black hole (Sgr A*) at the Milky Way's center (Understand)
4. Explain the "rotation curve problem" and why it implies the existence of dark matter (Apply)
5. Distinguish the disk population (young, metal-rich, ordered orbits) from the halo population (old, metal-poor, random orbits) (Analyze)

**Content Outline:**

1. **Opening hook:** "You've never seen the Milky Way from outside — no human has. Everything we know about our galaxy, we've figured out from the inside."

2. **Structure of the Milky Way:**
   - **Disk:** ~100,000 ly diameter, ~1,000 ly thick; contains gas, dust, young stars, spiral arms
   - **Bulge:** Central concentration of old, red stars; ~10,000 ly across
   - **Halo:** Sparse, spherical; old metal-poor stars + globular clusters
   - **Spiral arms:** Density wave theory (conceptual); sites of active star formation
   - Total mass: ~1–2 trillion M☉ (most of it dark matter)

3. **How we mapped it:**
   - William Herschel (1785): star counts → flattened shape, but wrong center
   - Harlow Shapley (1918): globular cluster distribution → Sun is NOT at center
   - **Cepheid variables:** Period-luminosity relation → distance indicator (Leavitt's discovery)
     - This is the bridge from Module 2: stellar evolution produces pulsating giants that become cosmic yardsticks
   - 21-cm hydrogen radio emission: traces spiral arms through dust

4. **The galactic center — Sgr A*:**
   - S-stars: orbits tracked over decades → 4 million M☉ in a region smaller than our solar system
   - 2020 Nobel Prize (Genzel & Ghez) — connects directly to L18 (black holes)
   - EHT image of Sgr A* (2022)

5. **Evidence for dark matter (introduction):**
   - **Rotation curves:** Plot orbital velocity vs. distance from center
     - Expected (visible mass only): $v$ should decrease with distance (Keplerian falloff)
     - Observed: $v$ stays roughly flat → much more mass than we can see
   - The missing mass = **dark matter** → surrounds the galaxy in a vast halo
   - This is just the Milky Way evidence — more in L21

**OMI Application:** Observable = stellar velocities at different radii. Model = Newtonian gravity ($v^2 = GM(r)/r$). Inference = enclosed mass → much more than visible → dark matter.

**Key Misconceptions:**
- "The Milky Way band in the sky IS the galaxy" → It's our edge-on view of the galactic disk
- "We're at the center of the galaxy" → We're ~26,000 ly from center, about halfway out
- "Dark matter is just stuff we haven't found yet" → It's not baryonic (normal matter) — addressed more in L21

**Connections:**
- Back: stellar evolution produces Cepheids (L16), Sgr A* black hole (L18)
- Forward: galaxy types (L20), dark matter evidence deepened (L21), Cepheids + Type Ia → distance ladder → Hubble's law (L22)

**OpenStax Reference:** Chapter 25 (The Milky Way Galaxy)

### L20 — Galaxy Zoo

**Date:** Wednesday, April 22
**Big Idea:** Galaxies come in a stunning variety — spirals, ellipticals, irregulars —
and their shapes tell us about their histories.

**Learning Objectives (Bloom's):**

1. Classify galaxies using Hubble's tuning fork diagram: ellipticals, spirals (normal and barred), irregulars (Apply)
2. Compare the typical properties (color, gas content, star formation) of elliptical vs. spiral galaxies (Analyze)
3. Explain how galaxy mergers and interactions shape galaxy evolution (Understand)
4. Describe active galactic nuclei (AGN) and quasars as powered by supermassive black hole accretion (Understand)
5. Explain how galaxy distances are measured beyond the Milky Way (Cepheids, Type Ia SNe, Hubble's law) (Understand)

**Content Outline:**

1. **Opening hook:** Hubble's discovery — galaxies are NOT nebulae within the Milky Way; they're entire "island universes" millions of light-years away

2. **Galaxy classification (Hubble tuning fork):**
   - **Ellipticals (E0–E7):** Round to elongated; old red stars, little gas/dust, low star formation
   - **Spirals (Sa–Sc, SBa–SBc):** Disk + arms + bulge; mix of old and young stars, active star formation in arms
   - **Barred spirals:** Bar structure through center (the Milky Way is SBbc)
   - **Irregulars:** No clear structure; often small, gas-rich (e.g., Magellanic Clouds)
   - **Lenticulars (S0):** Transitional — disk but no spiral arms

3. **Galaxy properties and what they tell us:**
   - Color → stellar population age (red = old, blue = young/star-forming)
   - Gas content → star formation potential
   - Shape → formation history (mergers vs. quiescent)

4. **Galaxy interactions and mergers:**
   - Tidal tails, bridges, starbursts triggered by close encounters
   - Major mergers: two spirals → elliptical (the "merger hypothesis")
   - The Milky Way + Andromeda → "Milkomeda" in ~4.5 billion years

5. **Active galaxies and quasars:**
   - AGN: supermassive black hole actively accreting matter → jets, enormous luminosity
   - Quasars: most luminous AGN; visible across the observable universe
   - Connects to L18: these are the supermassive BHs at galaxy centers, actively feeding
   - Unified model (brief): viewing angle determines whether we see a Seyfert, quasar, or blazar

6. **Measuring galaxy distances (the distance ladder continued):**
   - Cepheids (from L19): good to ~30 Mpc (Hubble's original method)
   - Type Ia supernovae (from L17): standardizable candle → good to billions of light-years
   - Hubble's law itself: once calibrated, redshift → distance (next lecture)

**OMI Application:** Observable = galaxy morphology, color, spectrum, redshift. Model = stellar populations, merger dynamics, AGN physics. Inference = formation history, central BH mass, distance.

**Key Misconceptions:**
- "Hubble's tuning fork is an evolutionary sequence" → It's a classification scheme, not a timeline
- "Elliptical galaxies are squished spheres" → They can be truly triaxial; the E number is projected shape
- "Quasars are stars" → The name means "quasi-stellar" but they're galactic nuclei at enormous distances

**Connections:**
- Back: Type Ia supernovae as standard candles (L17), supermassive black holes (L18), Cepheids (L19)
- Forward: dark matter in galaxy clusters (L21), Hubble's law and expansion (L22)

**OpenStax Reference:** Chapter 26 (Galaxies), Chapter 27 (Active Galaxies, Quasars)

### L21 — Dark Matter

**Date:** Monday, April 27
**Big Idea:** Most of the matter in the universe is invisible — we know it's there
only because of its gravitational effects, and after decades of searching, we still
don't know what it is.

**Learning Objectives (Bloom's):**

1. List at least four independent lines of evidence for dark matter (Remember)
2. Explain galaxy rotation curves and why they imply unseen mass (Apply — extends L19)
3. Describe gravitational lensing and how it reveals dark matter in galaxy clusters (Understand)
4. Explain why dark matter cannot be ordinary (baryonic) matter and list candidate particles (Understand)
5. Describe the large-scale structure of the universe (cosmic web: filaments, voids, clusters) and dark matter's role in shaping it (Understand)

**Content Outline:**

1. **Opening hook:** "95% of the universe is made of stuff we've never directly detected. This isn't speculation — the evidence is overwhelming."

2. **Evidence for dark matter (comprehensive):**
   - **Galaxy rotation curves** (review from L19, now generalized): flat rotation curves are universal, not just the Milky Way
   - **Galaxy cluster dynamics** (Zwicky, 1933): galaxies in clusters move too fast to be bound by visible mass alone
   - **Gravitational lensing:**
     - Strong lensing: arcs, multiple images of background galaxies
     - Weak lensing: statistical distortion of background galaxy shapes → mass maps
     - The Bullet Cluster: smoking gun — X-ray gas (normal matter) separated from mass (dark matter) during collision
   - **CMB power spectrum** (preview for L23): density fluctuations in early universe require dark matter

3. **What dark matter is NOT:**
   - Not gas, dust, or "dark" normal matter (ruled out by nucleosynthesis constraints)
   - Not black holes or stellar remnants (MACHOs: ruled out by microlensing surveys)
   - Not neutrinos (too light, too fast — "hot dark matter" doesn't form structures correctly)

4. **What it might be:**
   - WIMPs (Weakly Interacting Massive Particles): theoretically motivated, not yet detected
   - Axions: very light particles from particle physics theory
   - Direct detection experiments (underground detectors), collider searches (LHC) — nothing conclusive yet
   - "The dark matter problem is one of the biggest open questions in physics"

5. **Large-scale structure:**
   - The **cosmic web:** filaments of galaxies + dark matter, with voids between
   - Galaxy clusters at filament intersections
   - Dark matter provides the gravitational scaffolding; normal matter falls into DM halos
   - Simulations vs. observations: remarkable agreement → dark matter model works

**OMI Application:** Observable = lensed images of background galaxies. Model = general relativity (light bending by mass). Inference = total mass of lens → mostly dark matter.

**Key Misconceptions:**
- "Dark matter is just stuff we can't see because it's dark" → It doesn't interact with light at all (not absorb, not emit)
- "Dark matter is the same as dark energy" → Completely different: dark matter gravitates, dark energy drives expansion
- "We made up dark matter to fix our equations" → The evidence is independent and converging from many directions
- "We'll discover it soon" → Unknown — we've been looking for decades

**Connections:**
- Back: rotation curves (L19), galaxy clusters and mergers (L20), gravity (L6)
- Forward: dark energy is a separate mystery (L22), CMB constrains dark matter abundance (L23)

**OpenStax Reference:** Chapter 28.4 (The Challenge of Dark Matter)

### L22 — The Expanding Universe

**Date:** Wednesday, April 29
**Big Idea:** The universe is not static — it's expanding, and the expansion is
accelerating. This discovery reshaped our understanding of cosmic history and fate.

**Learning Objectives (Bloom's):**

1. Explain Hubble's discovery: galaxy redshifts increase proportionally with distance (Understand)
2. State Hubble's law ($v = H_0 d$) and use it to estimate distances from redshifts (Apply)
3. Explain what "expanding space" means and distinguish it from galaxies flying apart through space (Understand)
4. Describe how Type Ia supernovae revealed that the expansion is accelerating (Understand)
5. Define dark energy as the unknown cause of accelerating expansion and state its approximate contribution to the universe's energy budget (Understand)

**Content Outline:**

1. **Opening hook:** "In 1929, Edwin Hubble made one of the most profound discoveries in human history — the universe is getting bigger."

2. **Hubble's discovery:**
   - Slipher and Hubble: galaxy spectra are redshifted (almost universally)
   - Hubble (1929): plot of velocity (from redshift) vs. distance (from Cepheids) → linear relation
   - **Hubble's law:** $v = H_0 d$
     - $H_0 \approx 70$ km/s/Mpc (current best estimate — but the "Hubble tension" exists)
     - Worked example: galaxy at 100 Mpc → receding at ~7,000 km/s
   - What this means: the farther away, the faster it recedes → universe is expanding

3. **What is expanding?**
   - Space *itself* is stretching — galaxies are carried along like raisins in rising bread
   - Galaxies are NOT flying apart through space; the space between them is growing
   - Locally bound systems (galaxies, solar systems, atoms) don't expand — gravity holds them together
   - **Lookback time:** more distant = further back in time (connects to L1's "cosmic time machine" concept)
   - If the universe is expanding now, it was smaller in the past → extrapolate back → Big Bang

4. **The distance ladder completed:**
   - Parallax → Cepheids → Type Ia supernovae → Hubble's law
   - Each rung calibrates the next
   - Type Ia SNe: from Module 2 (L17) — WD exceeds Chandrasekhar limit → thermonuclear explosion → "standard candle"

5. **Accelerating expansion and dark energy:**
   - 1998 discovery: distant Type Ia supernovae are dimmer than expected → farther than expected → expansion is *speeding up*
   - Two independent teams (Perlmutter, Schmidt, Riess) → 2011 Nobel Prize
   - **Dark energy:** the unknown cause of accelerating expansion
     - ~68% of the total energy content of the universe
     - Cosmological constant? Vacuum energy? Something else? → open question
   - **Cosmic pie chart:** ~68% dark energy, ~27% dark matter, ~5% normal matter
     - "Everything you've ever seen, touched, or measured is 5% of the universe"

6. **The Hubble tension** (brief):
   - Local measurements of $H_0$ (Cepheids + SNe) and CMB-based measurements (Planck) disagree at ~5σ
   - Possible explanations: systematic errors? New physics? → active research frontier

**OMI Application:** Observable = redshift of galaxy spectrum + apparent brightness of Type Ia SN. Model = Hubble's law + standard candle. Inference = distance, recession velocity, expansion rate, acceleration.

**Key Misconceptions:**
- "The Big Bang happened at a specific location" → Everywhere was the same "point"; expansion is happening everywhere
- "Galaxies are moving through space" → Space is expanding; galaxies are carried along
- "We can see the edge of the universe" → We see a horizon set by the age of the universe × speed of light
- "Dark energy is the same as dark matter" → Different phenomena with different effects

**Key Calculations:**
- $v = H_0 d$ → given any two, find the third
- Rough age of universe: $t \approx 1/H_0 \approx 14$ Gyr (approximate)
- Distance from redshift (conceptual)

**Connections:**
- Back: Doppler effect / redshift (L9), Type Ia supernovae (L17), Cepheids (L19)
- Forward: if universe is expanding → it was smaller and hotter in the past → Big Bang (L23)
- Key bridge: Type Ia SNe taught as stellar physics (L17) are now revealed as the tool that discovered dark energy

**OpenStax Reference:** Chapter 26.5 (The Expanding Universe), Chapter 29.4 (The Expanding Universe)

### L23 — The Big Bang & the Fate of the Universe

**Date:** Monday, May 4
**Big Idea:** The universe had a beginning — hot, dense, and simple — and the evidence
is written in light that has been traveling for 13.8 billion years.

**Learning Objectives (Bloom's):**

1. List and explain three key pieces of evidence for the Big Bang: expansion, CMB, nucleosynthesis (Understand)
2. Describe the cosmic microwave background and explain why it is considered the strongest evidence for the Big Bang (Understand)
3. Outline a simplified timeline of the universe from the Big Bang to the present (Understand)
4. Explain how the balance between matter, dark matter, and dark energy determines the fate of the universe (Analyze)
5. Identify at least two major open questions in cosmology (Evaluate)

**Content Outline:**

1. **Opening hook:** "If the universe is expanding now, and we run the movie backwards, everything converges to a single hot, dense state. The evidence that this actually happened is written across the entire sky."

2. **Evidence for the Big Bang (three pillars):**

   a. **Cosmic expansion** (from L22): Hubble's law → extrapolate backwards → universe was smaller, hotter, denser

   b. **Cosmic microwave background (CMB):**
   - Predicted (Gamow, 1948): if the early universe was hot and dense, it should have cooled to a faint glow by now
   - Discovered (Penzias & Wilson, 1965): uniform microwave radiation from every direction, $T \approx 2.725$ K
   - Interpretation: this is the "afterglow" of the Big Bang, released ~380,000 years after the Big Bang when the universe cooled enough for atoms to form ("recombination") → photons could travel freely
   - COBE, WMAP, Planck: increasingly precise maps of tiny temperature fluctuations → seeds of galaxies
   - The CMB is the most perfect blackbody ever measured

   c. **Big Bang nucleosynthesis:**
   - In the first ~3 minutes: protons and neutrons fused to form light elements (H, He, small amounts of Li)
   - Prediction: ~75% H, ~25% He by mass, trace lithium
   - Observation: matches! This ratio is observed in pristine gas clouds and old stars
   - Heavier elements require stellar nucleosynthesis (connects back to Module 2)

3. **Cosmic timeline (simplified):**
   - $t = 0$: Big Bang — all energy in a hot, dense state
   - $t \sim 10^{-36}$ s: **Inflation** — brief, exponential expansion (solves horizon + flatness problems; brief, conceptual)
   - $t \sim 3$ min: **Nucleosynthesis** — light elements formed
   - $t \sim 380,000$ yr: **Recombination** — atoms form; CMB released; universe becomes transparent
   - $t \sim 200$ Myr: **First stars** — "cosmic dawn"; reionization begins
   - $t \sim 1–13$ Gyr: **Galaxy formation and evolution** — structures grow from small fluctuations
   - $t = 13.8$ Gyr: **Now** — you are here, made of atoms forged in stars

4. **The fate of the universe:**
   - Depends on the contest between gravity (slowing expansion) and dark energy (accelerating it)
   - **Current best model:** dark energy dominates → expansion accelerates forever → **"Big Freeze"**
     - Stars burn out, galaxies drift apart, universe cools toward absolute zero
   - Other (less likely) scenarios:
     - Big Crunch: gravity wins → universe recollapses (ruled out by acceleration data)
     - Big Rip: dark energy strengthens → tears apart galaxies, stars, atoms
   - The cosmological constant: simplest model for dark energy; consistent with data so far

5. **Open questions (grand finale):**
   - What is dark matter? (L21)
   - What is dark energy? (L22)
   - What happened before/during inflation?
   - Why is there more matter than antimatter?
   - Is the Hubble tension a clue to new physics?
   - Are we alone? (callback to L1 and the Drake equation from Module 1)

6. **Coda — Full circle:**
   - "In Lecture 1, we said astronomy is inference from constrained measurements — pretty pictures → measurements → models → inferences. Now you've traced that chain from a parallax angle to the age and fate of the entire universe."
   - "The iron in your blood was forged inside a star. The hydrogen in your water has been around since the Big Bang. You are the universe experiencing itself."

**OMI Application:** Observable = CMB temperature + tiny fluctuations. Model = Big Bang cosmology (expansion + cooling). Inference = age of universe (13.8 Gyr), composition (5/27/68 split), geometry (flat).

**Key Misconceptions:**
- "The Big Bang was an explosion in space" → It was an expansion *of* space; there's no center or edge
- "What was before the Big Bang?" → The question may not be well-defined; time itself began
- "The CMB comes from one direction" → It's everywhere, from every direction, uniformly
- "The universe is expanding into something" → Space itself is expanding; there's no "outside"

**Connections:**
- Back: Hubble's law (L22), Type Ia SNe (L17/L22), dark matter (L21), stellar nucleosynthesis (L17)
- Back to Module 1: lookback time (L1), light as information (L7)
- Culmination of the entire course's OMI framework

**OpenStax Reference:** Chapter 29 (The Big Bang)

> These are flagged for discussion and should be resolved before readings are written.

1. **Five lectures for galaxies + cosmology is tight.** The biggest risk is L23 (Big Bang + fate of universe) being overloaded. One option: move the "fate of the universe" material (Big Freeze, dark energy equation of state) into L22 as a coda, making L23 purely about evidence for the Big Bang + cosmic timeline.

2. **Module 3 has no standalone exam** — content goes directly to the comprehensive final. Should there be a short quiz or checkpoint (e.g., Friday of Week 15 before the review) to help students gauge readiness? Or is HW 10 + HW 11 sufficient?

3. **Dark matter gets a full lecture (L21) — is that warranted at the intro level?** Alternative: fold DM evidence into L19 (Milky Way) and L22 (cosmology), freeing a slot for a dedicated "cosmic evolution / fate" lecture. 4. **Galaxy classification depth.** L20 tries to cover the tuning fork, galaxy properties, mergers, AND active galaxies/quasars. That's a lot. Could cut active galaxies to a brief mention and save time, since AGN are less central to the cosmology story.

5. **Hubble tension.** Mentioned briefly in L22. Should this be expanded (it's a live research question and good for SLO 8 — evaluating claims), or kept minimal to avoid confusion at the intro level?
