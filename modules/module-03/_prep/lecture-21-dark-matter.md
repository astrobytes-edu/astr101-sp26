# Lecture 21: Dark Matter

## The Big Idea

> **[The Big Idea]**
>
> Most of the matter in the universe is invisible — we know it's there only because of its gravitational effects, and after decades of searching, we still don't know what it is. Yet dark matter's gravitational influence is *everywhere*: it holds galaxies together, binds galaxy clusters, and provides the scaffolding for cosmic structure itself. This is not speculation. It is among the best-supported conclusions in all of science, despite its strangeness.

---

## The Invisible Universe

Imagine discovering that 95% of your city doesn't emit light, doesn't reflect light, and doesn't interact with light in any detectable way. Yet you know it's there — not because you can see it, but because buildings collapse without it, planets don't orbit properly around it, and light bends around it. You'd call it a mystery. You'd spend decades searching for it. You'd propose increasingly exotic explanations.

This is the situation in astronomy. When we add up all the visible matter in the universe — stars, gas, dust, planets, and even black holes — we get about 5% of the total matter required to explain what we actually observe. The remaining 95% is *dark matter*: matter that does not interact with electromagnetic radiation, yet exerts a gravitational force we can measure.

*Margin: **Dark matter** — Matter that does not emit, reflect, or absorb light, detected only through its gravitational effects.*

This wasn't always clear. For most of the 20th century, astronomers assumed that what we could see was what existed. It took converging evidence from multiple independent sources — rotation curves, cluster dynamics, gravitational lensing, and large-scale cosmic structure — to force us to accept a radical conclusion: the universe is dominated by something we have never directly detected.

The story of dark matter is a masterclass in how science works. We observe something unexpected. We propose increasingly careful measurements. We test alternative explanations. We discover that no combination of ordinary matter can explain what we see. And we keep looking, because the mystery remains unsolved.

Let's trace the evidence.

---

## Line of Evidence #1: Galaxy Rotation Curves

You encountered rotation curves in Lecture 19. Here's the setup again, now viewed through the lens of dark matter.

A galaxy's rotation curve plots orbital velocity *v* as a function of distance *r* from the galactic center. For an object orbiting in a gravitational field, if all the mass is concentrated at the center (as it is approximately in our solar system), we expect orbital velocity to decline with distance:

$$v(r) \propto \sqrt{\frac{1}{r}}$$

This is *Kepler's law*. The farther out you orbit, the slower you move.

But here's what we actually observe in real galaxies:

**The rotation curves are *flat*.** From the inner disk out to the visible edge of a galaxy, orbital velocity stays roughly constant. At 10 kiloparsecs from the center, stars orbit at nearly the same speed as at 5 kiloparsecs. This was puzzling.

If orbital velocity remains constant with distance, that means the enclosed mass must increase linearly with distance. In the visible disk, mass increases (more stars farther out), but not nearly fast enough. We need *additional mass*, distributed in a roughly spherical halo extending far beyond the visible disk.

*Margin: **Rotation curve** — A plot of orbital velocity vs. galactocentric distance; reveals the distribution of mass in a galaxy.*

*Margin: **Dark matter halo** — A roughly spherical distribution of dark matter surrounding a galaxy, extending well beyond the visible disk.*

This dark matter halo is the key insight. It's not visible in starlight, but its gravity keeps the outer parts of the galaxy bound. Without it, the galaxy would fly apart.

> *Margin: **Kepler's law** — For a circular orbit in a gravitational field centered at a point, $v = \sqrt{GM/r}$, where $G$ is the gravitational constant, $M$ is enclosed mass, and $r$ is orbital radius.*

> **[Check Yourself 1]**
>
> A galaxy's outer stars orbit at 220 km/s. If we see enough visible matter to account for a circular orbit at 100 km/s only, how much *additional* mass (beyond visible matter) must be present?
>
> <details>
> <summary>Click to reveal</summary>
>
> Orbital velocity depends on $v = \sqrt{GM/r}$. If we need $v = 220$ km/s but visible matter provides only $v = 100$ km/s, the mass ratio is:
>
> $$\frac{M_{\text{total}}}{M_{\text{visible}}} = \left(\frac{220}{100}\right)^2 \approx 4.8$$
>
> So total mass is roughly 5 times visible mass. The "missing mass" is dark matter, making up roughly 4 out of 5 parts. This ratio varies with galaxy type, but the pattern is universal.
>
> </details>

> **[Key Insight]**
>
> Flat rotation curves are *not* peculiar to one galaxy or one type of galaxy — they are universal. Every spiral galaxy we've carefully measured has a flat rotation curve. Every elliptical galaxy's dynamics implies a dark matter halo. This universality is a clue: dark matter is not a local anomaly but a fundamental feature of galaxy formation itself.

> **[Misconception Alert]**
>
> *Misconception:* "Scientists made up dark matter to fix their equations."
>
> *Reality:* Galaxy rotation curves were measured carefully for decades before dark matter became accepted. Astronomers initially looked for alternative explanations (different gravity law, galactic warps, projection effects) before concluding that additional mass was required. Dark matter emerged from *observations*, not from theoretical preference.

> **[OMI Connection]**
>
> **Observable:** Orbital velocities of stars at different galactic radii (measured from Doppler shift of starlight).
>
> **Model:** Newton's laws of gravity (or general relativity at extreme precision) applied to circular orbits.
>
> **Inference:** Total enclosed mass as a function of radius. The discrepancy between inferred total mass and observed visible mass is dark matter.

## Line of Evidence #2: Galaxy Cluster Dynamics

While rotation curves revealed dark matter in individual galaxies, the real shock came from looking at *groups* and *clusters* of galaxies.

In 1933, the astronomer Fritz Zwicky was studying the Coma Cluster — a bound group of about 1,000 galaxies roughly 100 megaparsecs away. He measured the radial velocities (line-of-sight speeds) of individual galaxies in the cluster and applied the **virial theorem**.

The virial theorem relates kinetic energy and potential energy in a bound system. For a bound system at equilibrium:

$$2K + U = 0$$

where *K* is total kinetic energy and *U* is total gravitational potential energy. Rearranged:

$$2 \overline{\frac{1}{2}m v^2} = \frac{GM_{\text{total}}}{r}$$

In plain language: if you know the velocity dispersion (how fast galaxies jiggle around) and the system's size, you can calculate the total mass needed to hold it together.

*Margin: **Virial theorem** — In a gravitationally bound system at equilibrium, twice the kinetic energy equals the negative potential energy.*

Zwicky calculated the expected mass from the velocity dispersion of galaxies in the Coma Cluster. Then he counted all the visible galaxies and estimated their total mass from starlight. His result: the cluster needed about 400 times more mass than he could see. He called the invisible mass *dunkle Materie* — dark matter.

Most astronomers dismissed this result. Zwicky was known for bold claims. Perhaps he made an error. Perhaps the cluster wasn't actually bound. Perhaps the galaxies' velocities weren't representative.

But follow-up work in the 1970s and 1980s confirmed it. Galaxy clusters are "over-bound" — they contain far more mass than visible matter can account for. The virial theorem doesn't lie.

> *Margin: **Velocity dispersion** — A measure of how fast objects move in random directions around the center of a group or cluster.*

> **[Check Yourself 2]**
>
> Imagine a galaxy cluster where galaxies have an RMS velocity (root-mean-square velocity) of 1,000 km/s and a typical radius of 2 megaparsecs. Use the virial theorem to estimate the cluster's total mass. (Hint: $M \approx \frac{3v^2 r}{G}$ is a useful form.)
>
> <details>
> <summary>Click to reveal</summary>
>
> Plugging in numbers (with $G \approx 4.3 \times 10^{-3}$ pc/(M☉⋅km²/s²)):
>
> $$M \approx \frac{3 \times (1000 \text{ km/s})^2 \times 2 \times 10^6 \text{ pc}}{4.3 \times 10^{-3}} \approx 10^{15} M_\odot$$
>
> This is roughly $10^{15}$ solar masses. In a typical cluster, we might see stars and gas totaling only $10^{14}$ solar masses, leaving the remaining mass as dark matter.
>
> </details>

> **[Deep Dive 1: The Virial Theorem]**
>
> Why does the virial theorem work? Consider a spherical system of *N* particles, each with mass *m* and velocity *v*. The total kinetic energy is:
>
> $$K = \sum_i \frac{1}{2} m_i v_i^2$$
>
> The gravitational potential energy (assuming uniform density) scales as:
>
> $$U = -\alpha \frac{G M_{\text{total}}^2}{R}$$
>
> where $\alpha$ is a dimensionless constant and *R* is the system's size. For a system in dynamical equilibrium (not expanding or contracting), gravity balances pressure/velocity support:
>
> $$2K + U = 0$$
>
> This gives us $M_{\text{total}} \propto v^2 R / G$, independent of how the mass is distributed (as long as it's roughly uniform). This is why the virial theorem is so powerful — it requires only knowing velocities and size, not the detailed mass profile.

> **[Misconception Alert]**
>
> *Misconception:* "Maybe galaxies in clusters are just passing through, not really bound together."
>
> *Reality:* The virial theorem *defines* a bound system as one where $2K + U = 0$. If velocities are too high for the visible mass, the system isn't marginally bound — it's *over-bound*, implying additional mass. Tidal forces would have disrupted an unbound cluster long ago.

## Line of Evidence #3: Gravitational Lensing

Of all the evidence for dark matter, gravitational lensing is perhaps the most direct. Here's why: it doesn't depend on assumptions about equilibrium, velocity dispersion, or dynamical models. It's pure geometry and general relativity.

Einstein showed that massive objects bend spacetime, causing light rays to curve. The *deflection angle* depends only on the total mass of the lens, not on what kind of matter it is. This is the great virtue of lensing: it measures total mass directly.

*Margin: **Gravitational lensing** — The bending of light by the gravitational field of massive objects.*

There are three observational regimes:

### Strong Lensing: Multiple Images and Einstein Rings

When a distant source (a quasar, a distant galaxy) aligns perfectly behind a massive lens (a galaxy or galaxy cluster), we see *multiple images* of the source. The lens's gravity has bent the light rays so much that they take multiple paths to us.

<!-- FIGURE 3: Strong Lensing (Multiple Images and Einstein Ring)

   - Right panel: Einstein ring — a perfect circular arc of lensed light
   - Caption: Strong gravitational lensing. Left: The Einstein Cross, a quasar lensed into four images by an intervening galaxy. Right: An Einstein ring, formed when source and lens are perfectly aligned. Both are direct evidence of matter — dark or visible — along the line of sight.
-->

In some cases — when alignment is especially perfect — the source's light forms a complete ring around the lens. These are called *Einstein rings*.

The position of the multiple images depends on the mass distribution of the lens. By modeling the lens (assuming a smooth distribution), we can infer the total mass. Observations consistently find that the total mass inferred from lensing is much larger than the visible mass. The discrepancy is dark matter.

*Margin: **Einstein ring** — A complete or partial ring of light formed when a distant source is perfectly aligned behind a massive lens.*

> **[Check Yourself 3]**
>
> A distant quasar is gravitationally lensed by an intervening galaxy. We observe four images of the quasar. Would we expect the four images to have the same brightness?
>
> <details>
> <summary>Click to reveal</summary>
>
> No — the four light paths have different lengths and pass through different regions of the lens. Magnification (and thus brightness) depends on the convergence along each path. In the famous Einstein Cross (a real lensed quasar), the four images have different brightnesses. By measuring these brightness differences and the time delays between brightness variations in the quasar (caustics), we can constrain the lens's mass distribution and infer dark matter content.
>
> </details>

### Weak Lensing: Statistical Distortions

When there's no dramatic alignment, lensing is subtle. A distant galaxy's light is gently bent by the gravitational fields of structures along its path. Each individual distortion is small, but we can detect it statistically.

Weak lensing works like this: map the shapes of thousands of distant galaxies. Their shapes are randomly oriented initially. But the gravitational fields of massive structures along our line of sight *correlate* these shapes — galaxies on one side of a massive cluster appear slightly stretched tangentially. By measuring these small distortions (a few percent change in shape), we can map the dark matter distribution without assuming anything about galaxy dynamics or equilibrium.

This is a revolution in dark matter studies. Weak lensing has mapped dark matter in galaxy clusters with unprecedented precision and has revealed the large-scale cosmic web of dark matter structure.

> **[Key Insight]**
>
> Gravitational lensing is the most direct way to measure dark matter because it depends only on geometry and gravity, not on assumptions about equilibrium, velocity, or what the matter is made of. Whether dark matter is WIMPs, axions, or something entirely unknown, lensing measures its gravitational effect equally well.

### The Smoking Gun: The Bullet Cluster

In 2006, observations of the Bullet Cluster delivered the most direct evidence yet for dark matter as a distinct substance.

The Bullet Cluster formed when two massive galaxy clusters collided. During the collision:

- The *visible matter* (mostly hot gas in the intracluster medium) collided violently, like two cars crashing. It slowed down and piled up in the center, radiating X-rays.
- The *dark matter* passed through, barely interacting. Each cluster's dark matter halo continued moving forward, separating from the visible matter.

When we map the dark matter distribution (using weak lensing) and the visible matter distribution (using X-ray observations), we find them *spatially separated*. The dark matter is offset from the X-ray gas. This is a smoking gun: dark matter is not just "invisible gas." It's a different component that barely interacts with ordinary matter.

   - Caption: The Bullet Cluster. Red shows hot gas (visible matter) concentrated where the clusters collided. Blue shows dark matter (from lensing), offset from the gas. This separation proves dark matter is a distinct substance. (Data from Chandra X-ray Observatory and Hubble Space Telescope.)
-->

> **[OMI Connection]**
>
> **Observable:** Positions of distant galaxies, shapes of their images, X-ray emission from hot gas, and their spatial distribution.
>
> **Model:** General relativity (light bends around massive objects); hydrostatics (gas in gravitational equilibrium); dynamics of particle collision.
>
> **Inference:** Dark matter concentration maps and their distribution relative to visible matter. The Bullet Cluster's offset dark matter and gas is direct evidence that dark matter is not ordinary (baryonic) matter.

> **[Misconception Alert]**
>
> *Misconception:* "Gravitational lensing just shows there's *something* there — it could be ordinary matter that's just hard to see."
>
> *Reality:* True, lensing alone can't tell us *what* dark matter is. But combined with other evidence (rotation curves, cluster dynamics, primordial nucleosynthesis — see below), we can rule out ordinary baryonic matter. The Bullet Cluster is especially important because it shows dark matter is *spatially distinct* from visible matter, not just mixed in.

## What Is Dark Matter NOT?

Before asking what dark matter *is*, let's be clear about what it *cannot* be.

### Not Ordinary (Baryonic) Matter

Could dark matter simply be ordinary baryonic matter that doesn't emit light — cold gas clouds, dust, planets, black holes, neutron stars?

No, and here's why: we have an independent constraint from **Big Bang nucleosynthesis** (a topic previewed in Lecture 23).

In the first few minutes after the Big Bang, the universe was hot enough to forge light nuclei (deuterium, helium-3, helium-4, lithium-7). The abundance of these nuclei depends sensitively on the density of baryons (ordinary matter) in the early universe.

*Margin: **Baryonic matter** — Ordinary matter made of baryons (protons and neutrons), including atoms, molecules, gas, and stars.*

*Margin: **Big Bang nucleosynthesis** — The production of light nuclei (deuterium, helium, lithium) in the first few minutes after the Big Bang, which constrains the baryon density of the universe.*

Modern observations of primordial abundances (measured in old stars and in the intergalactic medium) tell us the total baryon density. Combined with measurements of the *total* matter density from rotation curves, cluster dynamics, and the cosmic microwave background, we conclude:

- Baryons: ~5% of the critical density
- Total matter (dark + baryonic): ~30% of the critical density
- Therefore, dark matter: ~25% of the critical density

In other words, dark matter outnumbers baryons by a factor of 5. There simply isn't enough "missing" ordinary matter to account for dark matter.

> **[Check Yourself 4]**
>
> If baryons make up 5% of the universe's total density, and dark matter makes up ~25%, what is the ratio of dark matter to baryonic matter?
>
> <details>
> <summary>Click to reveal</summary>
>
> $$\frac{\text{Dark Matter}}{\text{Baryons}} = \frac{0.25}{0.05} = 5$$
>
> For every 5 kg of dark matter in the universe, there is 1 kg of ordinary matter. This is a striking fact: we are made of the universe's minority component!
>
> </details>

### Not MACHOs (Massive Compact Halo Objects)

In the 1990s, some astronomers proposed that dark matter might be ordinary matter in the form of compact objects: brown dwarfs, black holes, neutron stars, or white dwarfs. These were called **MACHOs**.

MACHOs were searched for using **microlensing**. When a MACHO passes in front of a distant star, its gravity bends the star's light, causing a brief brightening. The duration and shape of the light curve reveal the MACHO's mass.

*Margin: **MACHO** — Massive Compact Halo Object; a proposed dark matter candidate consisting of stellar remnants or brown dwarfs.*

*Margin: **Microlensing** — Gravitational lensing by a compact object, causing a distant star to brighten temporarily.*

Extensive MACHO searches found some candidates, but nowhere near enough to account for all the dark matter. Moreover, nucleosynthesis constraints rule out the required baryon density. MACHOs were abandoned as a serious dark matter candidate around 2000.

## What Is Dark Matter? The Particle Candidates

If dark matter is not ordinary matter, what is it?

This is *the* open question. Here are the leading candidates:

### WIMPs (Weakly Interacting Massive Particles)

A WIMP is a hypothetical particle that:
- Has mass (hence "massive")
- Interacts only through gravity and the weak nuclear force (hence "weakly interacting")
- Does not interact electromagnetically

*Margin: **WIMP** — Weakly Interacting Massive Particle; a hypothetical dark matter candidate that couples only to gravity and the weak force.*

WIMPs were a natural dark matter candidate because they arise in certain extensions of the Standard Model of particle physics. In the early, hot universe, WIMPs would have been produced in abundance. As the universe expanded and cooled, most WIMPs would have annihilated into lighter particles — but a residual density would remain today.

The predicted relic abundance of WIMPs, calculated from particle physics models, matches the observed dark matter density. This coincidence — called the "WIMP miracle" — made WIMPs extremely popular.

For decades, experiments have searched for WIMPs directly: looking for the rare collision of a WIMP with an atomic nucleus in an underground detector. So far, no confirmed detection.

> **[Deep Dive 2: The WIMP Miracle]**
>
> In the early universe, WIMPs (and their antiparticles) were created and destroyed in equal numbers. As the universe cooled below the WIMP mass ($m_\chi c^2$), creation shut off, but annihilation continued until the WIMP number density fell so low that annihilation became rare. The *comoving number density* froze out.
>
> The relic abundance today is:
>
> $$\Omega_\chi h^2 \propto \frac{1}{\sigma_a}$$
>
> where $\sigma_a$ is the annihilation cross-section. By measuring the observed dark matter density and solving for the cross-section, we find $\sigma_a \approx 10^{-26}$ cm³/s — exactly what naive particle physics models predict! This is the "WIMP miracle": no fine-tuning needed.
>
> However, decades of direct and indirect searches have not confirmed WIMPs, leading many to reconsider alternatives.

### Axions

An axion is a hypothetical, extremely light particle arising from a proposed solution to the "strong CP problem" in particle physics. Axions are so light they're almost ghost-like — a single axion has negligible mass, but there could be $10^{80}$ of them in the observable universe.

*Margin: **Axion** — Hypothetical ultralight particle, extremely abundant, proposed to solve problems in particle physics and potentially compose dark matter.*

Axions barely interact with ordinary matter. They pass through detectors without a trace. But in a strong magnetic field, axions can convert to photons (and vice versa). This is the principle behind modern axion detection experiments like ADMX.

### Other Candidates

- **Sterile neutrinos:** Hypothetical particles that interact only through gravity, undetected so far.
- **Primordial black holes (PBHs):** Black holes formed in the early universe from density fluctuations, not stellar collapse. Recent gravitational wave detections have revived interest in PBHs as dark matter.

> **[Misconception Alert]**
>
> *Misconception:* "We'll probably discover dark matter particles in the next few years."
>
> *Reality:* Dark matter searches have been remarkably unsuccessful for 30+ years despite billions in funding and extraordinary experimental ingenuity. It's possible dark matter is something we haven't yet thought of — a particle beyond the Standard Model, or even a modification of gravity itself (though most evidence favors genuine matter). This is humbling, and it's OK to say: we don't know.

> **[Misconception Alert]**
>
> *Misconception:* "Dark matter and dark energy are the same thing."
>
> *Reality:* Completely different. Dark matter is matter (has mass, clusters, responds to gravity like ordinary matter). Dark energy is not matter; it has negative pressure and causes cosmic acceleration. We'll explore dark energy in Lecture 22.

## Large-Scale Structure: The Cosmic Web

Zoom out from galaxies and clusters. Look at the universe on the largest scales — the distribution of all matter and galaxies across billions of light-years.

What we find is not random. Galaxies are not uniformly scattered. Instead, they form a vast **cosmic web**: long filaments of galaxies separated by vast empty voids.

This structure — filaments and voids — is not a surprise. It emerges naturally from gravity acting on tiny density fluctuations in the early universe. The densest regions collapse into filaments; these filaments intersect at high-density nodes (galaxy clusters). The least-dense regions remain mostly empty.

*Margin: **Cosmic web** — The large-scale structure of the universe consisting of galaxy filaments separated by cosmic voids, all embedded in dark matter.*

Here's the key point: **dark matter provides the gravitational scaffolding**. It's far more abundant than ordinary matter, so its distribution dominates gravity. Ordinary matter collects in the deepest gravitational wells carved out by dark matter. Galaxies form where dark matter is densest.

This is why dark matter is so important for understanding cosmic structure. We can't understand why galaxies exist, why they're arranged the way they are, or how large-scale structure formed, without accounting for dark matter's dominant gravity.

> **[Key Insight]**
>
> The cosmic web is not "just the distribution of galaxies." Galaxies are passengers on dark matter's gravitational structure. The filaments are dark matter; the clusters are dark matter; even the void boundaries are shaped by dark matter. We live in a universe whose architecture is built by matter we've never directly detected.

> **[OMI Connection]**
>
> **Observable:** The distribution of galaxies (from large surveys like SDSS, 2dF, and others mapping millions of galaxies); the patterns of large-scale structure.
>
> **Model:** Gravitational instability in an expanding universe; N-body simulations of gravity and dark matter particle motion (complex, requiring supercomputers).
>
> **Inference:** The distribution and clustering of dark matter. Simulations that include both dark matter and baryonic physics match observations only when dark matter is included with the inferred abundance.

> **[Check Yourself 5]**
>
> In the cosmic web, galaxies are more densely clustered along filaments than in voids. What does this tell us about dark matter distribution?
>
> <details>
> <summary>Click to reveal</summary>
>
> Since galaxies follow gravity (ordinary matter collects in the deepest gravitational wells), the concentration of galaxies along filaments implies dark matter is also concentrated along filaments. If dark matter were uniformly distributed, galaxies would be too (on average). The cosmic web traces the dark matter distribution directly.
>
> </details>

## The Observable Universe's Composition

Let's take stock. Modern cosmology has determined the composition of the universe to remarkable precision, mostly from the cosmic microwave background (Lecture 23) and large-scale structure:

| Component | Density Parameter | Fraction |
|-----------|-------------------|----------|
| Dark energy | $\Omega_\Lambda \approx 0.68$ | 68% |
| Dark matter | $\Omega_m \approx 0.27$ | 27% |
| Ordinary (baryonic) matter | $\Omega_b \approx 0.05$ | 5% |
| **Total** | $\Omega_{\text{tot}} \approx 1.00$ | **100%** |

*Margin: **Density parameter (Ω)** — The ratio of a component's density to the critical density of the universe.*

This composition is astonishing: 95% of the universe is not ordinary matter. Of that, 27 percentage points are dark matter — invisible but gravitationally dominant. And 68 percentage points are dark energy — something even stranger that we'll discuss next lecture.

We are made of a minority component. We live on the least common type of matter in the universe. This should be humbling.

## Summary

Dark matter is one of astronomy's greatest mysteries, but the evidence for its existence is overwhelming:

1. **Galaxy rotation curves:** Stars orbit galaxies too fast for visible matter alone to hold them in. Dark matter halos, extending well beyond visible disks, provide the needed gravity.

2. **Galaxy cluster dynamics:** Galaxies in clusters move too fast to be bound by visible matter. The virial theorem reveals total masses 10–100 times larger than visible mass.

3. **Gravitational lensing:** Both strong lensing (multiple images, Einstein rings) and weak lensing (statistical distortions of galaxy shapes) reveal that total mass far exceeds visible mass. The Bullet Cluster proves dark matter is spatially distinct from ordinary matter.

4. **Large-scale structure:** The cosmic web of galaxy filaments and voids traces the distribution of dark matter, the universe's gravitational scaffolding.

Dark matter is **not** ordinary baryonic matter — nucleosynthesis constraints and microlensing searches rule this out. The leading candidates are **WIMPs** (hypothetical heavy particles) and **axions** (ultralight particles), but after decades of searching, neither has been confirmed.

Despite its mystery, dark matter's effects are everywhere: it holds galaxies together, binds clusters, seeds cosmic structure, and dominates the universe's gravity. The search continues.

## Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Axion** | Hypothetical ultralight particle, extremely abundant, proposed to solve particle physics problems and potentially compose dark matter. |
| **Baryonic matter** | Ordinary matter made of baryons (protons and neutrons), including atoms, molecules, gas, and stars. |
| **Big Bang nucleosynthesis** | The production of light nuclei (deuterium, helium, lithium) in the first few minutes after the Big Bang; constrains the baryon density of the universe. |
| **Cosmic web** | The large-scale structure of the universe consisting of galaxy filaments separated by cosmic voids, all embedded in dark matter. |
| **Dark matter** | Matter that does not emit, reflect, or absorb light; detected only through its gravitational effects. Comprises ~27% of the universe's total density. |
| **Dark matter halo** | A roughly spherical distribution of dark matter surrounding a galaxy, extending well beyond the visible disk. |
| **Density parameter (Ω)** | The ratio of a component's density to the critical density of the universe; values sum to ~1 in a flat universe. |
| **Einstein ring** | A complete or partial ring of light formed when a distant source is perfectly aligned behind a massive gravitational lens. |
| **Gravitational lensing** | The bending of light by the gravitational field of massive objects. Occurs in three regimes: strong lensing (multiple images), weak lensing (statistical), and microlensing. |
| **Kepler's law** | For a circular orbit in a gravitational field centered at a point, $v = \sqrt{GM/r}$, where $G$ is the gravitational constant, $M$ is enclosed mass, and $r$ is orbital radius. |
| **MACHO** | Massive Compact Halo Object; a proposed dark matter candidate consisting of stellar remnants or brown dwarfs (now largely ruled out). |
| **Microlensing** | Gravitational lensing by a compact object, causing a distant star to brighten temporarily as the lens passes in front of it. |
| **Rotation curve** | A plot of orbital velocity vs. galactocentric distance; reveals the distribution of mass in a galaxy. Flat rotation curves imply dark matter halos. |
| **Virial theorem** | In a gravitationally bound system at equilibrium, $2K + U = 0$, where $K$ is total kinetic energy and $U$ is total gravitational potential energy. Used to infer total mass from velocity dispersion. |
| **WIMP** | Weakly Interacting Massive Particle; a hypothetical dark matter candidate that couples only to gravity and the weak nuclear force. |
