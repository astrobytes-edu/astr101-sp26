# The Big Bang and the Fate of the Universe

## The Big Idea

> **[The Big Idea]**
> The universe had a beginning — hot, dense, and simple — and the evidence is written in light that has been traveling for 13.8 billion years. By running the cosmic expansion backward and measuring the ancient light of the early universe, we have reconstructed the biography of everything.

---

## Running the Movie Backward

Imagine you are watching a film of the universe, and you hit rewind. What happens?

From Lecture 22, you know that galaxies are moving away from us — the farther away they are, the faster they recede. This is Hubble's law: the universe is expanding. Now imagine reversing the motion. If everything is spreading apart *now*, then yesterday everything was closer together. A week ago, even closer. A thousand years ago? Much closer. A million years ago? Nearly touching.

Keep rewinding. Keep imagining denser, hotter conditions. Ten million years ago. A hundred million. A billion. The universe becomes a furnace. Atoms are ripped apart into plasma — electrons torn from nuclei. Go back further. Nuclei themselves shatter. Protons and neutrons roam freely in a sea of radiation. Keep going. Go back to the first microseconds of time itself.

At the very beginning — at *t* = 0 — the movie runs backward into infinity. All the matter and energy of the universe, all 100 billion galaxies, all the stars and planets and atoms, compressed into a single, infinitely hot, infinitely dense point.

This state is called the **Big Bang**. Not an explosion in space, but the explosion *of* space itself — the beginning of expansion, of time, of the universe.

This is not metaphor or speculation. It is the modern scientific conclusion, built on three pillars of observational evidence so strong that they have convinced nearly every astrophysicist on Earth. In this lecture — the final one of ASTR 101 — we will trace how we know this, what it means, and what it tells us about the future.

> *Margin:* **Big Bang**: The state of extremely high temperature and density in the early universe, and the name of the theoretical model explaining the universe's origin and evolution.

## Pillar 1: Cosmic Expansion

The first piece of evidence was discovered by Edwin Hubble (Lecture 22) and is the simplest: *we observe the expansion happening right now*.

From Hubble's law, the recession velocity *v* of a galaxy is proportional to its distance *d*:

$$v = H_0 \cdot d$$

Here, $H_0$ is the **Hubble constant** — the slope of the expansion. The current best estimate is $H_0 \approx 70 \text{ km/s/Mpc}$. This means that for every megaparsec of distance, a galaxy moves 70 km/s farther away.

Now, reverse it. If objects are separating at a constant rate (dictated by $H_0$), when were they together? A simple calculation gives:

$$t = \frac{d}{v} = \frac{d}{H_0 \cdot d} = \frac{1}{H_0}$$

This is called the **Hubble time**, and it is roughly the age of the universe:

$$t_{\text{age}} \approx \frac{1}{H_0} \approx 14 \text{ billion years}$$

(The exact value is 13.8 billion years — slightly younger than the Hubble time because the expansion has accelerated due to dark energy, but the order of magnitude is correct.)

The logic is irresistible: if the universe is expanding now, then it was smaller in the past. Extrapolate backward, and you reach a moment when everything was at the same place. The universe has been expanding ever since.

But expansion alone does not prove the Big Bang model. You need to see the *echo* of those early, hot conditions.

> *Margin:* **Hubble constant** ($H_0$): The slope of the relationship between galaxy recession velocity and distance; approximately 70 km/s/Mpc today.

## Pillar 2: The Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB)

The second pillar is one of the most stunning discoveries in science: the **cosmic microwave background**.

### A Prediction Made in 1948

In 1948, a young physicist named George Gamow made an audacious prediction. If the universe was once incredibly hot — a furnace of particles and radiation — then even as it cooled and expanded, that ancient light should still be here. It would be redshifted to long wavelengths (the expansion stretches light waves), but it should fill the entire sky as a faint, cold glow.

Gamow's colleagues thought he was wild. But he was right.

### The Discovery: Penzias and Wilson, 1965

In 1964, two radio engineers at Bell Labs — Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson — were trying to reduce noise in a new antenna. They kept detecting a faint hiss, a microwave signal coming equally from every direction. At first, they thought it was equipment malfunction. They even cleaned pigeon droppings from the antenna (a fact proudly recorded in the acknowledgments of their paper). But the signal persisted.

By 1965, they realized what they were hearing: the afterglow of the Big Bang. This was the cosmic microwave background — ancient light released roughly 380,000 years after the Big Bang, when the universe had cooled enough for electrons and protons to combine into atoms (the **recombination era**). Before that, the universe was opaque; light could not travel freely. After recombination, the universe became transparent, and the light has been traveling toward us ever since — now redshifted into the microwave region of the spectrum.

Penzias and Wilson won the Nobel Prize in 1978.

> *Margin:* **Cosmic microwave background (CMB)**: Radiation left over from ~380,000 years after the Big Bang, now appearing as microwaves (wavelength ~1 mm) filling the entire sky at a temperature of 2.725 K.

### The CMB's Temperature: A Perfect Blackbody

When you measure the spectrum of the CMB — the intensity at each wavelength — it is a *perfect blackbody* at a temperature of approximately **2.725 Kelvin**. (More precisely, 2.72548 ± 0.00057 K — one of the most precisely measured numbers in all of science.)

A blackbody spectrum is what you expect from an object in thermal equilibrium. The fact that the CMB matches a blackbody spectrum so perfectly is extraordinary evidence that the early universe was in thermal equilibrium — a hot, dense, homogeneous gas of radiation and particles. This is exactly what Big Bang theory predicts.

### Maps of Temperature Fluctuations: Seeds of Galaxies

The overall temperature of the CMB is 2.725 K, but when you map it across the sky with exquisite precision — using satellites like COBE (1989–1993), WMAP (2001–2010), and Planck (2009–2013) — you find tiny variations, called **anisotropies**. These are temperature fluctuations of only about 1 part in 100,000 (roughly ±0.00001 K).

These minuscule variations are profound. They are the seeds from which galaxies grew. A region slightly denser than average would have stronger gravity and would attract more matter. A region slightly underdense would lose matter. Over billions of years, these tiny variations grew — through gravitational amplification — into the vast filaments and voids of the cosmic web (Lecture 21).

The pattern of these fluctuations carries a fossil record of the early universe. The sizes of the hot and cold spots encode the geometry of the universe (is it flat? curved?), the amount of matter and dark matter, and the amount of dark energy. By analyzing these patterns, cosmologists can determine fundamental properties of the cosmos with remarkable precision.

> **[Key Insight]**
> The cosmic microwave background is the strongest evidence for the Big Bang because:
> 1. It exists — exactly as predicted before it was found.
> 2. It is a perfect blackbody — proof of thermal equilibrium in the early universe.
> 3. Its tiny temperature fluctuations match predictions from inflation theory.
> 4. These fluctuations match the distribution of galaxies we observe today — a stunning consistency check.

## Pillar 3: Big Bang Nucleosynthesis

The third pillar of evidence comes from the first three minutes of the universe — before stars formed, before galaxies formed, before even neutral atoms formed. It is called **Big Bang nucleosynthesis (BBN)**.

### The First Three Minutes

In the first fraction of a second, the universe was too hot for nuclei to exist — protons and neutrons were free particles in a sea of radiation and other particles. As the universe expanded, it cooled. By about 1 second, the temperature had dropped to about 10 billion Kelvin — cool enough that neutrons and protons could stick together to form light nuclei: deuterium (a proton and a neutron), helium-3, helium-4, and tiny traces of lithium-7.

This process — fusion of protons and neutrons into light nuclei — is nuclear synthesis. It happened not in the cores of stars, but in the universe at large, in the first few minutes of time. By the end of BBN (roughly 3 minutes after the Big Bang), nearly all the free neutrons had been incorporated into helium nuclei. Protons remained protons — these are the nuclei of hydrogen, which dominates to this day.

The predicted outcome of BBN is surprisingly simple:

- Roughly **75% of all ordinary matter** (by mass) is hydrogen.
- Roughly **25%** is helium.
- Traces (parts per 10 billion) of lithium, beryllium, and boron.

These predictions come from detailed calculations involving nuclear physics and the thermal history of the early universe.

> *Margin:* **Big Bang nucleosynthesis (BBN)**: The formation of light nuclei (H, He, Li) in the first few minutes of the universe through nuclear fusion of protons and neutrons.

### The Observational Test: Matching Pristine Gas

How do we test this prediction? We cannot go back in time. But we can find regions of gas that have never been processed by stars.

In the spectra of certain quasars, we see the light passing through clouds of primordial gas — gas that formed shortly after the Big Bang and has never undergone stellar nucleosynthesis. By measuring the absorption lines of hydrogen and helium in these clouds, astronomers can measure the primordial abundance of helium.

The result: **approximately 24–25% helium by mass**. This matches the BBN prediction to within measurement uncertainty.

Moreover, the prediction of BBN depends on the **baryon density** (the density of ordinary matter like protons and neutrons) and the number of types of neutrinos. By comparing the predictions with observations of primordial abundances, we can constrain these parameters. The results are consistent with independent measurements from the CMB anisotropies and from supernovae (used to measure dark energy).

This consistency is a powerful test. Three independent methods — primordial abundances, CMB anisotropies, and supernovae — all point to the same values for the density and composition of the universe. This agreement is not a coincidence. It is evidence that the Big Bang model is correct.

> **[Deep Dive]**
> ### Why Do We See 75% Hydrogen and 25% Helium, and Not Some Other Mix?
>
> This depends on the ratio of protons to neutrons at the start of BBN. Early in the universe, protons and neutrons were in equilibrium — they could convert into each other through weak nuclear interactions. But as the universe cooled, this equilibrium froze out. The ratio that became "locked in" was roughly 7 protons for every 1 neutron.
>
> Why 7:1? Because the neutron is slightly more massive than the proton (by about 1.3 MeV/$c^2$, corresponding to a temperature of about 1.3 billion Kelvin). At colder temperatures, it becomes less favorable to produce neutrons, so fewer neutrons survive.
>
> During BBN, nearly all neutrons pair up with protons to form helium-4 (two protons + two neutrons). The remaining protons stay as hydrogen. With 7 protons for every neutron that gets locked in:
> - Total mass in nucleons = 7 + 1 = 8 (in units where the neutron = 1)
> - Mass in helium = 2 × (neutron) = 2 (each helium has 2 protons + 2 neutrons, but we care about the neutrons)
> - Actually: 1 neutron locked in → $^4$He with 1 proton. So 1 neutron → 1 unit of helium.
> - Remaining protons → hydrogen
>
> The exact ratio depends on nuclear reaction rates, the neutron lifetime, and the baryon density. Remarkably, all of these are well-understood from laboratory nuclear physics. BBN is a quantitative prediction, not a hand-wavy estimate.

> **[Check Yourself]**
> <details>
> <summary>Why do we not see heavy elements like iron and carbon in primordial gas clouds?</summary>
>
> Heavy elements require temperatures higher than existed during BBN, or they are created through a different process: stellar nucleosynthesis (Lecture 17). In the first three minutes of the Big Bang, the temperature dropped rapidly. By 3 minutes, it had cooled to roughly 1 billion Kelvin — cool enough that nuclear synthesis stopped. At this temperature, fusion cannot create nuclei heavier than lithium (which requires three nucleons).
>
> All the carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, silicon, iron, and heavier elements we see today were created inside stars over billions of years. This is why older stars (from the early universe) have lower abundances of heavy elements than younger stars. Every heavy atom in your body was forged inside a star and scattered into space by a supernova — a fact worth pausing to appreciate.
> </details>

## The Cosmic Timeline: From Big Bang to Now

Let us now stitch together the evidence into a coherent history of the universe. This is the **cosmic timeline**.

| **Time After Big Bang** | **Temperature** | **Major Event** |
|---|---|---|
| $t = 0$ | Infinite | Big Bang — all matter and energy in infinitely dense state |
| $t \sim 10^{-43}$ s (Planck time) | $10^{32}$ K | Physics unknown (quantum gravity needed) |
| $t \sim 10^{-36}$ s | $10^{27}$ K | **Inflation** — exponential expansion (hypothetical; explains horizon problem) |
| $t \sim 1$ μs | $10^{13}$ K | Quark-gluon plasma forms protons and neutrons |
| $t \sim 3$ min | $10^9$ K | **Big Bang nucleosynthesis** — H, He, Li form; BBN ends |
| $t \sim 380,000$ yr | $3,000$ K | **Recombination** — electrons bind to nuclei; universe becomes transparent; **CMB released** |
| $t \sim 50$ Myr | $\sim$ 100 K | First small density fluctuations collapse into gas clouds |
| $t \sim 100$–$200$ Myr | $\sim$ 10 K | **Cosmic dawn** — first stars ignite |
| $t \sim 1$ Gyr | Cool | First galaxies assemble |
| $t \sim 3$ Gyr | Cool | Peak of star formation (lots of starbursts and mergers) |
| $t \sim 13.8$ Gyr | $2.725$ K | **Now** — present day (CMB cooled by expansion; stars fading; acceleration due to dark energy) |

The timeline reveals the grand narrative: from simplicity to complexity, from homogeneous gas to the intricate structure of galaxies, from a universe dominated by radiation to one dominated by dark matter to one now dominated by dark energy.

> **[Key Insight]**
> The timeline is not pure speculation. Each milestone is grounded in observations:
> - Expansion rate ($H_0$) → age of the universe
> - CMB temperature and anisotropies → density of matter, dark matter, dark energy
> - Galaxy ages from stellar populations → age of first galaxies
> - Abundances of light elements → BBN predictions
> These independent methods all converge on an age of 13.8 ± 0.04 billion years.

> **[Check Yourself]**
> <details>
> <summary>Why do we say "13.8 billion years ago" instead of "14 billion years ago" even though the Hubble time is ~14 billion years?</summary>
>
> The Hubble time $1/H_0$ assumes the expansion rate has been constant. In reality, the expansion has *not* been constant. In the early universe, gravity slowed the expansion (deceleration). But about 5–6 billion years ago, dark energy took over and began *accelerating* the expansion. So the universe is younger than the naive Hubble time predicts. The precise age (13.8 billion years) comes from fitting the entire expansion history — using the CMB, supernovae, and other measurements — not just from $H_0$ alone.
> </details>

## The Fate of the Universe: Past and Future

We have reconstructed the past. Now, what about the future?

The fate of the universe depends on a contest between two forces: **gravity** (which pulls matter together, tending to slow expansion) and **dark energy** (a mysterious component that accelerates expansion).

### The Matter Density Parameter: $\Omega_m$

Let us define $\Omega_m$ as the *matter density parameter* — the ratio of the universe's actual matter density to the "critical density," a threshold value that divides different fates:

$$\Omega_m = \frac{\rho_{\text{matter}}}{\rho_c}, \quad \text{where} \quad \rho_c = \frac{3H_0^2}{8\pi G}$$

If $\Omega_m > 1$ (matter density exceeds critical), gravity dominates and the universe will eventually recollapse (**Big Crunch**).

If $\Omega_m < 1$ (matter density falls short), expansion wins and the universe expands forever (**Big Freeze**).

If $\Omega_m = 1$ exactly, the universe is *flat* — a critical case.

### Dark Energy and Acceleration

But there is a wrinkle. Around 1998, astronomers studying Type Ia supernovae (Lecture 17) made a shocking discovery: the expansion of the universe is *accelerating*. This was a complete surprise. Gravity should be slowing things down. Instead, something was speeding them up.

That something is **dark energy** — a form of energy permeating all of space, exerting a repulsive gravitational effect. We do not know what dark energy is. It could be the **cosmological constant** (Λ, a constant energy density in vacuum), or it could be something more exotic. But its effects are undeniable.

Define $\Omega_\Lambda$ as the dark energy density parameter:

$$\Omega_\Lambda = \frac{\rho_{\text{dark energy}}}{\rho_c}$$

The current best measurements from the CMB (Planck satellite) and supernovae give:

- $\Omega_m \approx 0.30$ (matter and dark matter combined)
- $\Omega_\Lambda \approx 0.70$ (dark energy)
- $\Omega_m + \Omega_\Lambda \approx 1.00$ (universe is flat)

> *Margin:* **Dark energy**: A mysterious component of the universe that accelerates expansion, comprising ~68% of the current total energy density. Its nature is unknown.

### Three Possible Fates

Given the current measurements, three scenarios are possible:

1. **Big Freeze (most likely, ~95% probability)**: Dark energy continues to dominate. The expansion accelerates forever. Over trillions of years, stars exhaust their fuel and dim. Galaxies drift apart. Black holes evaporate via Hawking radiation. The universe becomes cold, dark, and empty — a vast expanse of elementary particles and radiation at temperatures near absolute zero.

2. **Big Crunch (ruled out)**: If dark energy were actually repulsive gravity (i.e., $\Omega_\Lambda < 0$), or if dark energy decayed away, the universe would eventually recollapse and end in infinite temperature and density. Current observations rule this out with high confidence.

3. **Big Rip (unlikely, ~5% probability)**: If dark energy increases in strength with time (rather than staying constant), it could eventually tear apart everything — galaxies, stars, atoms, nucleons themselves. This requires dark energy to violate certain physical constraints, but it is not impossible.

The most likely scenario — the Big Freeze — is both awesome and melancholy. The universe will not end in a bang. It will fade into darkness over timescales so vast that the trillion-year lifetime of the Sun is a mere blink.

> **[Misconception Alert]**
> *"The Big Bang was an explosion that happened at one place, with everything flying away from that place into empty space."*
>
> This is wrong. The Big Bang did not explode *in* space — it was an explosion *of* space itself. There was no center and no boundary. Space was everywhere, and everywhere was expanding. Imagine the universe as the *surface* of an inflating balloon. The 2D creatures on that surface see their neighbors moving away, but there is no "center" on the surface itself — the center is in the higher dimension (the interior of the balloon, which we cannot see). Similarly, our 3D universe is expanding in a higher dimension; there is no center within our space.

> **[Misconception Alert]**
> *"The Big Bang happened 13.8 billion years ago somewhere in space; the light from it has been traveling toward us."*
>
> This is also wrong. The Big Bang did not happen at one location. It happened *everywhere* at once. Every point in the universe today came from a causally connected region in the Big Bang. The light we see now — the CMB — was released from the region that became *our local region* after expansion and cooling. We are not observing the Big Bang happening at a distant location; we are observing light from a time when the universe was much younger and much hotter everywhere.

## Open Questions: The Frontiers of Cosmology

The Big Bang model is remarkably successful. It explains the expansion, the CMB, and the abundances of light elements. But it leaves profound mysteries unsolved — questions that will drive cosmology for decades to come.

### 1. What is Dark Matter?

Dark matter comprises 27% of the universe's energy density (or 85% of all matter). We know it exists because of its gravitational effects (Lecture 21): rotation curves of galaxies, gravitational lensing, structure formation. But we do not know what it is. The leading candidates are weakly interacting massive particles (WIMPs), axions, and primordial black holes. Experiments are searching for direct collisions between dark matter particles and ordinary nuclei. So far, the search has been fruitless — dark matter is even more elusive than expected.

### 2. What is Dark Energy?

Dark energy comprises 68% of the universe's energy density, yet we have no idea what it is. The simplest explanation is the **cosmological constant** (Λ), a constant energy density inherent to the vacuum. But the cosmological constant has a theoretical problem: when you calculate its value from quantum field theory, you get an answer that is ~120 orders of magnitude too large. This is one of the worst predictions in the history of physics. Other theories propose that dark energy is a dynamic field that evolves with time (**quintessence**), but no consensus has emerged.

### 3. What Happened During Inflation?

In the first ~$10^{-36}$ seconds, the universe underwent exponential expansion called **inflation**. This solves two deep problems: (1) Why is the universe so flat? (2) Why does the CMB have the same temperature in all directions, even though different regions have never been in causal contact?

Inflation answers these by stretching the universe by a factor of ~$10^{26}$, making it flat and homogeneous. The tiny CMB anisotropies come from quantum fluctuations, magnified by inflation to macroscopic scales. This is a beautiful idea — but is it true? What caused inflation? What stopped it? These questions remain open.

### 4. Matter-Antimatter Asymmetry

In the Big Bang, matter and antimatter should have been created in equal amounts. When they collided, they would have annihilated completely, leaving only radiation. Yet here we are — a universe made of matter, not antimatter. Why? This is called the **matter-antimatter asymmetry problem**, and it remains unsolved. Some process in the early universe must have created a tiny excess of matter over antimatter, but we do not know what it was.

### 5. The Hubble Tension

Recent measurements of $H_0$ from nearby galaxies (using Cepheid variables and supernovae) give values around 73 km/s/Mpc. But measurements from the CMB (using the standard Big Bang model) give around 67 km/s/Mpc. This 9% discrepancy, called the **Hubble tension**, is larger than the combined measurement uncertainties. Either one method is wrong, or something is wrong with the standard model. If the latter, it could point to new physics beyond the Big Bang cosmology we have described.

## Deep Dive: Inflation and the Horizon Problem

> **[Deep Dive]**
> ### Why Is the Universe So Uniform? The Horizon Problem.
>
> Look at the cosmic microwave background. It has the same temperature in every direction — to a precision of 1 part in 100,000. Yet regions on opposite sides of the sky are separated by a distance of ~27.5 billion light-years (the diameter of the observable universe). In the 13.8 billion years since the Big Bang, light can travel only 13.8 billion light-years. So how can opposite sides of the sky have ever "communicated" to reach the same temperature?
>
> This is the **horizon problem**. Without inflation, there is no solution.
>
> Inflation provides an answer: In the first $10^{-36}$ seconds, the universe underwent exponential expansion, doubling its size every $10^{-36}$ seconds or so. This expansion was so rapid that even tiny regions at the Big Bang could expand to become the size of the observable universe today. What began as a small, homogeneous region (in causal contact) inflated to cosmic scales. By the time inflation ended, the entire observable universe had been in contact long enough to equilibrate to the same temperature.
>
> Inflation predicts that the universe should be flat (which it is: $\Omega_{\text{total}} \approx 1$). It also predicts that the tiny CMB anisotropies should come from quantum fluctuations of the inflaton field (the hypothetical field driving inflation), stretched to macroscopic scales. These predictions have been tested by measuring the spectrum and statistics of the CMB anisotropies — and they are confirmed.
>
> But inflation raises new questions: What is the inflaton field? What caused it to start inflating? What stopped it? These remain open.

## Connecting Back to Lecture 1: The Power of Inference

Recall Lecture 1. We began with a simple observation: stars have parallax. From that tiny angle, we inferred distances. From distances and brightnesses, we inferred luminosities and temperatures. From stellar spectra, we inferred composition. From binary orbits, we inferred masses. Each step was a rung on a ladder of inference, building from constrained measurements to grand conclusions.

In this lecture, we have traced the ultimate inference: from the expansion of galaxies, from the echo of ancient light (the CMB), and from the abundances of light elements, we have inferred the origin and fate of the *entire universe*.

We have learned that:

- The universe had a beginning — not eternal, but created 13.8 billion years ago.
- That beginning was not gradual but sudden, hot, and dense — the Big Bang.
- The universe was once incomprehensibly different from today: it was small, hot, simple, and homogeneous.
- Over billions of years, gravity built up complexity: atoms, stars, galaxies, and the cosmic web.
- The future is written, if dark energy persists: a vast, cold, dark, empty expanse lasting trillions of years.

> **[Misconception Alert]**
> *"What was before the Big Bang?"*
>
> This is perhaps the most common question, and it rests on a misconception. Time itself is part of the universe. According to general relativity, the Big Bang was not a moment in time; it was the *beginning* of time. There is no "before" the Big Bang, any more than there is a latitude south of the South Pole. The question is not ill-defined; it is asking for something that does not exist.
>
> Some cosmologists speculate about "pre-Big Bang" physics, cyclic universes, or quantum fluctuations that might explain the origin of the Big Bang itself. But these are highly speculative and beyond the scope of modern observational cosmology.

> **[Misconception Alert]**
> *"The universe is expanding into empty space."*
>
> No. Space itself is expanding. There is no "empty space" outside the universe for it to expand into. The universe contains everything. When we say "space is expanding," we mean that the metric (the measurement of distance) is increasing. Distance between galaxies increases, but not because they are moving through pre-existing space — they are at rest in space, and space is stretching between them.

## From Stardust to Stars: The Iron in Your Blood

Here is a profound thought to end with.

Every atom in your body heavier than hydrogen was forged inside a star. The carbon in your proteins, the calcium in your bones, the iron in your blood — all created in stellar furnaces. When that star died in a supernova, those atoms were scattered into space and, over billions of years, incorporated into the cloud of gas and dust that became our solar system, our Earth, and eventually *you*.

The hydrogen in your water is older still — it has been around since the Big Bang, present in the universe since the first three minutes of creation.

You are made of stardust. You are the universe experiencing itself.

## Summary

The Big Bang theory — that the universe had a hot, dense beginning 13.8 billion years ago — rests on three pillars of evidence:

1. **Cosmic expansion**: Galaxies are moving apart. Extrapolating backward, they converge to a single point roughly 14 billion years ago.

2. **The cosmic microwave background**: The universe is filled with ancient light released 380,000 years after the Big Bang, when it became transparent. This light has been redshifted to microwave wavelengths and forms a perfect blackbody at 2.725 K. Its tiny anisotropies seed the galaxies.

3. **Big Bang nucleosynthesis**: In the first three minutes, fusion created a universe that is ~75% hydrogen and ~25% helium. This prediction matches observations of primordial gas.

From these, we have reconstructed the cosmic timeline from $t = 0$ (Big Bang) to $t = 13.8$ billion years (now). We have learned that the universe's fate depends on dark energy, which currently dominates and will drive an eternal, accelerating expansion (the Big Freeze).

Major open questions remain: What is dark matter? What is dark energy? What triggered inflation? Why is there more matter than antimatter?

The Big Bang model is a triumph of human reasoning — we have understood the origin of the universe through observation, inference, and mathematics. And it has revealed a humbling truth: we live in a vast cosmos, 13.8 billion years old, expanding toward an infinite, cold, dark future. Yet within that cosmic immensity, on a small planet orbiting an ordinary star, conscious beings emerged to ponder the cosmos and their place in it.

## Practice Problems

### Core Problems

1. **The Hubble Time.** The Hubble constant is $H_0 = 70 \text{ km/s/Mpc}$. Calculate the Hubble time (age of the universe, ignoring dark energy acceleration) in billions of years. Compare to the current best estimate (13.8 Gyr). Why is the universe younger than the Hubble time?

2. **CMB Temperature from Redshift.** The cosmic microwave background was released at a temperature of roughly 3,000 K, when the universe was about 380,000 years old. Today, we observe it at 2.725 K. This cooling is due to the expansion (redshift) of the universe. If the scale factor was $a = 0.001$ when the CMB was released (380,000 yr after Big Bang) and $a = 1$ today, what was the redshift $z = (1 - a)/a$? (Note: this is approximate; real calculations account for the scale factor evolution.)

3. **Baryon Density from BBN.** In Big Bang nucleosynthesis, the fraction of protons converted to helium nuclei depends on the baryon density. If the baryon density is very high, more neutrons survive (because they decay more slowly relative to the expansion rate), and more helium is produced. Conversely, low baryon density produces less helium. The observed primordial helium abundance of ~25% by mass constrains the baryon density to $\Omega_b h^2 \approx 0.022$ (where $h$ is the normalized Hubble constant). Explain qualitatively why a higher baryon density leads to higher primordial helium abundance.

4. **Dark Energy Dominance.** Current measurements give $\Omega_m = 0.30$ and $\Omega_\Lambda = 0.70$. This means that the present-day energy density of dark energy is 2.3 times that of all matter. At what lookback time (in the past) was the dark energy density equal to the matter density? (Hint: Matter density scales as $\rho_m \propto (1 + z)^3$; dark energy density remains roughly constant in the standard model.)

5. **Future of the Universe.** If dark energy remains constant and dominates forever, the scale factor of the universe grows as $a(t) \propto e^{Ht}$ for large $t$, where $H$ is the Hubble parameter in the far future. Using the second law of thermodynamics and the principle that the maximum entropy is achieved when all energy is evenly distributed, argue why the Big Freeze scenario leads to maximum entropy in the infinite future.

### Challenge Problems

6. **The Flatness Problem (Advanced).** The universe appears to be flat ($\Omega_{\text{total}} \approx 1$) to exquisite precision. In the early universe without inflation, the density parameter would naturally deviate from 1 exponentially as the universe aged: $|\Omega - 1| \propto e^{t/t_{\text{Hubble}}}$. For the universe to be flat today ($|\Omega - 1| < 10^{-15}$), the initial density would have had to be tuned to absurd precision at $t = 1$ s. Inflation solves this by making the universe so large that $\Omega \approx 1$ naturally. Explain (in words, no equations needed) why inflation makes flatness a prediction rather than an coincidence.

7. **Constraints on Dark Energy from CMB and Supernovae (Advanced).** The positions of the acoustic peaks in the CMB power spectrum depend on the geometry of the universe, which is determined by $\Omega_{\text{total}} = \Omega_m + \Omega_\Lambda$. Independently, the luminosity distances to high-redshift supernovae depend on the expansion history, which depends on both $\Omega_m$ and $\Omega_\Lambda$ separately. Explain (conceptually) how combining these two probes allows us to determine both $\Omega_m$ and $\Omega_\Lambda$ individually, rather than only their sum.

8. **Nucleosynthesis and the Baryon Asymmetry (Advanced).** In the Big Bang, pairs of protons and antiprotons, neutrons and antineutrons, were created and annihilated in equal numbers. Yet the universe today is made of matter. The asymmetry is tiny: for every 10 billion antimatter particles, there were 10 billion + 1 matter particles. This "plus one" in 10 billion is enough to leave a residue of matter. Given that roughly 84% of the baryonic matter in the universe is hydrogen and 16% is helium (by number; 75% and 25% by mass), explain why the matter-antimatter asymmetry must have arisen *before* Big Bang nucleosynthesis (i.e., before the first three minutes). What would have happened if the asymmetry arose *after* BBN?

## Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Anisotropy** | Variation in a property (like temperature) in different directions. CMB anisotropies are tiny temperature fluctuations across the sky. |
| **Baryon** | A subatomic particle made of three quarks (e.g., proton, neutron). Baryonic matter is ordinary matter (atoms). |
| **Big Bang** | The origin of the universe ~13.8 billion years ago, in an extremely hot and dense state. Not an explosion in space, but the beginning of space and time. |
| **Big Bang Nucleosynthesis (BBN)** | The formation of light nuclei (H, He, Li) in the first ~3 minutes of the universe. |
| **Blackbody** | An idealized object that absorbs all radiation falling on it and emits radiation in a characteristic spectrum determined only by its temperature (Planck spectrum). |
| **Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB)** | Radiation left over from ~380,000 years after the Big Bang, now observable as microwaves throughout the entire sky at a temperature of 2.725 K. |
| **Cosmological Constant (Λ)** | A constant energy density of the vacuum, proposed by Einstein. Current evidence suggests it comprises ~68% of the universe's energy density (dark energy). |
| **Critical Density** | The density that separates an expanding universe from one that recollapses; $\rho_c = 3H_0^2 / (8\pi G)$. |
| **Dark Energy** | A mysterious form of energy that comprises ~68% of the universe's energy density and drives the accelerating expansion. Its nature is unknown. |
| **Dark Matter** | Invisible matter comprising ~27% of the universe's energy density. Detected only through gravitational effects; its composition is unknown. |
| **Density Parameter** | The ratio of the actual density of a component (matter, dark energy) to the critical density. Denoted $\Omega_m$, $\Omega_\Lambda$, etc. |
| **Hubble Constant** ($H_0$) | The slope of the relation between galaxy recession velocity and distance; ~70 km/s/Mpc today. |
| **Hubble Time** | $1/H_0$; roughly the age of the universe if the expansion rate had been constant. ~14 billion years. |
| **Inflation** | A hypothetical period of exponential expansion in the very early universe (~$10^{-36}$ s), which explains the flatness and uniformity of the universe. |
| **Nucleosynthesis** | The formation of atomic nuclei from protons and neutrons. Occurs in stellar interiors (stellar nucleosynthesis) and in the early Big Bang (primordial nucleosynthesis). |
| **Recombination** | The era (~380,000 years after the Big Bang) when electrons and protons combined into neutral hydrogen atoms, allowing the universe to become transparent. |
| **Redshift** | The shift of light to longer (redder) wavelengths due to the expansion of space. Quantified as $z = \Delta\lambda / \lambda_0$. |
