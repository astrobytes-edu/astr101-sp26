# ASTR 101 — NotebookLM Infographic Design Prompts for Pedagogical Figures

Copy/paste these prompts into NotebookLM when you want polished, pedagogically effective astronomy infographics that feel visually intentional rather than generic.

These prompts are written for **introductory college astronomy** and are especially useful for **Module 2 / Lecture 14: The Sun**, but the style system can be reused for other lectures.

---

## How to Use These

1. Start with the **Reusable Style Prefix**.
2. Then paste one of the figure-specific prompts below it.
3. If needed, add one extra sentence naming your preferred output style:
   - "Make this a clean textbook infographic."
   - "Make this feel like a premium science museum panel."
   - "Make this visually dramatic but still classroom-readable."

The goal is not just pretty art. The goal is a figure that helps students notice the right pattern, ask the right question, and remember the core idea.

---

## Reusable Style Prefix

```text
Create a polished educational infographic for an introductory college astronomy course. Make it visually striking, scientifically accurate, and easy to read. Use a clean modern textbook-meets-museum style: deep space palette or dark background, high-contrast labels, warm gold/orange for energy, blue/cyan for plasma and atmosphere, clear arrows, minimal clutter, strong visual hierarchy, and student-friendly annotations. Include a short built-in “What to notice” caption directly in the figure. Prioritize pedagogy over decoration. Labels should be large and legible. The figure should feel elegant, concept-first, and scientifically serious.
```

## 1. Anatomy of the Sun

```text
Create an educational infographic showing the anatomy of the Sun as a labeled cutaway. Include the core, radiative zone, convective zone, photosphere, chromosphere, and corona. Show the Sun as a cross-section with clear color-coded layers. Add short labels explaining each layer’s main job:

- Core: fusion generates energy
- Radiative zone: photons diffuse outward slowly
- Convective zone: hot plasma rises, cool plasma sinks
- Photosphere: visible surface
- Chromosphere: thin active atmosphere
- Corona: hot outer atmosphere extending into space

Add a small Earth for scale and label key solar properties nearby: radius, mass, luminosity, surface temperature, and age. Include a built-in caption that says: “What to notice: the Sun is layered because different physical jobs happen in different regions.” Make it elegant, readable, and visually beautiful.
```

## 2. Proton-Proton Chain and Mass Defect

```text
Create a pedagogical infographic explaining the proton-proton chain in the Sun. Show the three main reaction steps visually:

1. Two protons fuse to make deuterium, a positron, and a neutrino
2. Deuterium captures a proton to make helium-3 and a photon
3. Two helium-3 nuclei fuse to make helium-4 and return two protons

Also include a clear mass-defect panel showing:
- mass before: four protons
- mass after: one helium-4 nucleus
- the small missing mass labeled as converted into energy with E = mc^2

Make the diagram scientifically accurate but visually intuitive. Use arrows, particle icons, and a simple “mass before -> mass after -> energy released” flow. Include a built-in caption: “What to notice: the Sun shines because a tiny amount of mass is converted into enormous energy.” Make it feel like a premium science museum infographic.
```

## 3. Solar Granulation as Evidence for Convection

```text
Create a close-up educational infographic of solar granulation on the Sun’s photosphere. Show the surface as a textured pattern of bright granular cells and darker boundaries. Add arrows showing hot plasma rising in the bright centers and cooler plasma sinking in the darker edges. Label the pattern as granulation and note that each granule is about 1000 km across.

Include a small side annotation that explains:
- bright center = hot upwelling plasma
- dark boundary = cooler sinking plasma
- this is visible evidence of convection

Add a built-in caption: “What to notice: convection is not abstract — we can actually see its surface signature on the Sun.” Make it beautiful, highly legible, and concept-first rather than photorealistic.
```

---

## 4. Spörer Butterfly Diagram

```text
Create a visually compelling educational infographic of the Spörer butterfly diagram for the solar cycle. Show a graph with time on the horizontal axis and solar latitude on the vertical axis from +90 degrees to -90 degrees. Plot many sunspots as dark dots. At the start of each cycle, place dots at higher latitudes around 30 to 40 degrees north and south. As time progresses, make the dots appear closer and closer to the equator, forming the classic butterfly-wing pattern.

Label key regions: solar minimum, rising phase, solar maximum, declining phase. Include a short annotation explaining that the pattern shows the Sun’s magnetic cycle is structured, not random.

Add a built-in caption: “What to notice: sunspots do not appear anywhere at random — their latitude drifts systematically during the solar cycle.” Make it elegant and data-driven, with a strong astronomy-infographic feel.
```

## 5. Gravity Inward, Pressure Outward

```text
Create a conceptual astronomy infographic showing the Sun as a balance between inward gravity and outward pressure. Show a cutaway Sun or spherical diagram with arrows pointing inward labeled gravity and arrows pushing outward labeled pressure. In the center, show fusion in the core as the source of the pressure support. Make the visual story clear:

gravity inward -> fusion releases energy -> pressure outward -> temporary stability

Add a small note that if pressure drops, the Sun contracts, heats up, and fusion increases again. This should visually communicate hydrostatic equilibrium and the stellar thermostat.

Include a built-in caption: “What to notice: a star survives by maintaining a temporary truce between gravity and pressure.” Make it feel bold, elegant, and intellectually satisfying.
```

## 6. Why E = mc^2 Matters for Stars

```text
Create an educational infographic explaining why E = mc^2 matters for stars. Show four protons on one side, a helium nucleus on the other, and a small amount of missing mass turning into photons, neutrinos, and heat. Include a simple visual scale showing that a tiny amount of mass corresponds to a huge amount of energy because c^2 is enormous.

Add a small section connecting this to special relativity:
- mass is a form of energy
- fusion converts some rest mass into usable energy
- this is why stars can shine for billions of years

Add a brief human-energy connection:
- fusion is attractive because of huge energy density
- the Sun gets free confinement from gravity
- on Earth fusion is hard because we must create and confine an ultra-hot plasma ourselves

Include a brief scientifically careful note that room-temperature “cold fusion” is an aspirational idea, but accepted practical fusion research still requires extreme conditions. Built-in caption: “What to notice: special relativity is not abstract here — it explains why stars shine.” Make it pedagogical, beautiful, and not overly technical.
```

## 7. Neutrinos as Messengers from the Core

```text
Create an educational infographic explaining solar neutrinos for an introductory astronomy course. Show the Sun in cross-section with photons zig-zagging slowly outward through the interior while neutrinos travel almost straight out from the core. Label neutrinos as electrically neutral, extremely low-mass particles that interact only through the weak nuclear force. Show that they escape the Sun almost immediately compared with photons, which take a very long random-walk journey outward.

Add a comparison panel:
- photons tell us about energy transport
- neutrinos tell us what is happening in the core right now

Include a small note about the solar neutrino problem and its resolution through neutrino oscillations. Built-in caption: “What to notice: neutrinos let us test the hidden core of the Sun directly.” Make the figure feel elegant, explanatory, and discovery-driven.
```

## 8. Full Lecture 14 Synthesis Figure

```text
Create a one-page summary infographic for an introductory astronomy lecture about the Sun. Organize the graphic around one throughline: “The Sun is a temporary truce between gravity and pressure.”

Include five linked panels:
1. Gravity inward vs pressure outward
2. Fusion in the core powers pressure support
3. Radiation and convection transport energy outward
4. Magnetism creates sunspots, flares, and coronal activity
5. Neutrinos and helioseismology let us test the hidden interior

Use arrows to connect the panels as one causal chain. Keep text minimal and concept-rich. Add one short built-in caption at the bottom: “What to notice: the Sun is not just the nearest star — it is the star whose hidden interior we can test most directly.” Make it visually unified, elegant, and worthy of a course homepage or lecture slide.
```

```text
Keep the figure clean and diagrammatic, not painterly.
```

```text
Do not make this look like children’s science art; make it look like a college-level instructional graphic.
```

```text
Use fewer visual effects and more explanatory structure.
```

```text
Make the labels large, sparse, and pedagogically purposeful.
```

```text
Favor clear causal arrows and evidence-based annotations over decorative space backgrounds.
```
